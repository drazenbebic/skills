# Game Dialogue Lives or Dies in Context

Professionals write game dialogue as action under constraint. Each line is a short move one character makes to get something from another, and it should do at least two jobs at once: carry information, reveal character, or shift the relationship. What shape a line takes depends less on what it says than on what triggers it, how often the player will hear it, who speaks it, and whether it will be voiced, subtitled, translated or cut off mid-sentence. Game writers take their core technique from screenwriting and theatre (McKee's dialogue as verbal action, Mamet's "who wants what," Johnstone's status games). They take compression from comics and subtitling (about 25 words per speech balloon, and two subtitle lines of 38–42 characters each), and quick NPC sketching from tabletop game-mastering. On top of that they add what only games need: branching and state tracking, pools of barks, budgets for reactivity, and spreadsheet-driven voice-over and localization pipelines. The best-documented failures were invisible on the page and obvious in play. Skyrim's guard line was heard to death. God of War Ragnarök's companions gave puzzle hints within seconds. Fallout 4's dialogue labels did not match what the hero then said. Forspoken's modern snark clashed with its fantasy setting. That is why serious review simulates the running game instead of rereading the script. Large language models fail in predictable ways on exactly the skills this craft values. In expert-judged studies their fiction lacks subtext, explains emotions outright, flattens voices and names into the same few defaults, and softens villains, and the models cannot reliably judge their own writing. An agent that writes game dialogue should therefore copy the professional pipeline: take in a character and context brief, classify the line type, design the structure and state, draft from want, tactic and status, apply hard production limits, then critique against concrete, checkable tests and hand the output over as drafts for a human writer. Many commonly quoted numbers, such as how many variants a bark needs, how many words fit in a node, or how long before a first hint, have no published standard. This report marks where guidance rests on practitioner heuristics rather than evidence.

## Eighteen terms a newcomer needs before anything else

Game dialogue is nonlinear, repeated, and assembled by software at runtime, so the writer's deliverable is not a screenplay. It is a database of lines, each with an ID, a speaker, a trigger and metadata. Rafael Chandler argued as early as 2005 that screenplay format suits linear cinematics and fits in-game dialogue poorly, and that the working document should be a sortable spreadsheet ([Chandler, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/organizing-and-formatting-game-dialogue)). The scale explains why. Baldur's Gate 3 holds the Guinness record for the longest videogame script at **2,503,837 words**, of which 2,121,425 are character dialogue ([Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/764696-longest-script-for-a-videogame)). Star Wars: The Old Republic recorded more than **200,000 voiced lines** ([Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/98119-largest-voice-over-project-in-the-entertainment-industry)). Supergiant shipped more than **22,000 fully voiced lines** in Hades with a team of fewer than 20 people ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1026975/Breathing-Life-into-Greek-Myth)). The vocabulary below recurs through the rest of this report.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Line | One unit of speech or text, normally one row in the script database with its own stable ID |
| NPC / PC | Non-player character / player character (the protagonist the player controls) |
| Bark | A short line an NPC speaks when a game event fires (spotting the player, reloading, taking a hit) ([Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7Cm07zbBGy4Xml6WgYi25d/the-convergence-of-ai-and-creativity-introducing-ghostwriter)) |
| Ambient or crowd chatter | Background lines NPCs say to each other or to themselves so the world feels inhabited |
| Banter / interjection | Companions talking among themselves, or cutting into a conversation, when context allows |
| Efforts | Non-verbal vocal sounds (grunts, gasps, death cries) recorded alongside dialogue |
| VO | Voice-over, the recorded performance. The VO script is usually a spreadsheet |
| Pickup | A line recorded in a later session to fix or extend earlier recordings |
| Hub-and-spoke | A conversation in which the player picks topics from a menu and returns to it |
| Branch-and-bottleneck (foldback) | Branches that rejoin at shared story beats, with their differences carried forward as variables |
| State, flag, variable | Stored facts about what has happened, read later to decide which lines play |
| Reactivity | How far lines respond to the player's past choices and present situation |
| Storylet | A self-contained piece of content with prerequisites and effects, offered whenever its conditions hold |
| Salience-based dialogue | A system that picks, from a pool, the line whose conditions best match the current world state |
| Skill check | A stat test that gates a dialogue option. Passive checks fire automatically, active checks are rolled |
| Paraphrase / dialogue wheel | A short option label standing in for a longer line the voiced PC will speak |
| Subtitles vs. captions | Subtitles render speech. Captions also render important non-speech sound ([XAG 104](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/104)) |
| Concatenation | Building a sentence from code fragments, the most common way dialogue breaks in translation |

Two more terms come up often in criticism. *Diegetic* text exists inside the fiction, such as a note on a desk, rather than in the interface. *Ludonarrative dissonance*, Clint Hocking's 2007 coinage about BioShock, names the conflict that arises when the story asks for one set of values and the mechanics reward another ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/ludonarrative-dissonance-meaning-video-games-origins-definition)).

## Good lines do two jobs while someone wants something

The most consistent standard in the game-writing literature is that a line has to work on several levels at once. Evan Skolnick's *Video Game Storytelling* holds that every line should carry at least one kind of exposition, ideally two or more, while still sounding natural. Emily Short, endorsing that standard, diagnoses boring dialogue as dialogue that **fails to do enough at once**; her fix is to add character or emotional content rather than more information ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2017/07/04/video-game-storytelling-evan-skolnick/)). Ian Adams puts it as a cutting rule: any line that does not deliver exposition, motivation or characterization goes, and lines that can be merged should be ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/6-tips-for-writing-video-game-dialogue-that-s-actually-funny)). The priority among those jobs leans toward character. In "Death to the Three Act Structure!" at the **2014** GDC Narrative Summit, Tom Abernathy and Richard Rouse III argued that players remember characters far more than plot, and that praise for a game's "story" usually means its people ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-plot-is-overrated-game-narrative-is-all-about-your-characters)). David Kuelz's rebuttal accepted the emphasis but warned against abandoning plot to get it ([Game Developer](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/a-rebuttal-to-quot-death-to-the-three-act-structure-quot-)). The constraint behind all of this is the player's attention. Skolnick contrasted Grand Theft Auto III, which puts the player in control after **under three minutes** of exposition, with Metal Gear Solid 2, which runs **nearly thirteen minutes** of cutscenes and radio calls first, most of it information the player never needed ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-evan-skolnick-asks-game-writers-to-make-it-snappier-)). Even bit parts get a point of view. Mark Slabinski insists that unnamed NPCs need a name and an attitude, and his worked quest-giver is a cowardly, miserly, theatrical farmer whose motive is protecting his family from wolves ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)).

The engine underneath every good line is a want. Robert McKee's *Dialogue* (2016) treats each line as a verbal tactic: beneath it the writer builds a desire, an intent and an action ([McKee Story](https://mckeestory.com/?p=10332)). He separates the **said** (what a character chooses to express), the **unsaid** (thoughts voiced only inwardly) and the **unsayable** (urges the character cannot name even privately) ([Kirkus](https://www.kirkusreviews.com/book-reviews/robert-mckee/dialogue-art)). David Mamet's memo to the writers of *The Unit* reduces scene diagnosis to three questions: who wants what, what happens if they don't get it, and why now. The popular phrasing "who wants what from whom" is a later paraphrase ([SlashFilm](https://www.slashfilm.com/a-letter-from-david-mamet-to-the-writers-of-the-unit/)). Keith Johnstone's improv work adds *status*. Status here means the pecking-order position people claim through behavior, not their social class, and it is negotiated constantly in every conversation. One of the quickest ways to raise your own status is to lower someone else's, and when the status is right a scene largely plays itself ([Antoine Buteau on Johnstone](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-keith-johnstone/)). Subtext is then the gap between what a character wants and the words they choose to get it. On-the-nose dialogue closes that gap by having people announce their feelings, motives or the theme.

Jon Ingold of inkle turned these ideas into game practice at AdventureX 2018. He showed an early NPC exchange from Assassin's Creed Odyssey and argued that it does no dramatic work: names and places are repeated and nothing changes between the speakers. His model was the owl scene in *Blade Runner*, which he read as a contest for power carried out through dialogue ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/developer-shows-how-to-write-good-game-dialogue-using-b-1830797912); [Robert Yang](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html)). His key adaptation for interactive work is to **fix the subtext and vary the text**. The writer locks the underlying power struggle as structure and lets the player choose the surface wording, so the player feels present without being able to break the scene ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/developer-shows-how-to-write-good-game-dialogue-using-b-1830797912)). A constructed illustration of the difference: the on-the-nose version is "I'm angry that you left us at the bridge," answered with "I feel guilty about it." The subtextual version is "Bridge held, by the way. In case you wondered," answered by a character who sets down a bottle and says only "Brought you something. Keep it." The anger now arrives as sarcasm and the guilt as a peace offering, and neither is named.

### Scenes enter on the want and leave on an open question

A conversation is a scene in miniature. It has a want, an obstacle and a turn, and something must be different at the end. A Game Developer guide to NPC dialogue tells writers to skip formulaic introductions and small talk and go straight to the point, the game version of "enter late" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/worldbuilding-with-npc-dialogue-a-beginner-s-guide)). The common attribution of "enter late, leave early" to William Goldman could not be verified. For exits, Slabinski recommends what he calls the *LOST school*: for every question answered, raise two more, and give players reason to doubt NPCs' motives. In his example, the farmer asks for a ring from the wolf leader's body before a count arrives, which raises the questions of why a wolf has a ring and why a peasant can pay so well ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Hannah Nicklin's dialogue workshop rests on one exercise: a character tries to persuade another to do something trivial, such as lending a book, and the game version ends on a player choice of **one to five words** ([Nicklin](https://gutefabrik.com/craft-writing-better-dialogue-a-2-day-workshop)). At larger scale, Abernathy and Rouse argued that episodic television suits games better than three-act film structure, citing the self-contained episodes of Telltale's *The Walking Dead* tied together by the Lee and Clementine relationship ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/-plot-is-overrated-game-narrative-is-all-about-your-characters)). Skolnick similarly observed that a typical game is almost all "Act 2" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-evan-skolnick-asks-game-writers-to-make-it-snappier-)). Short identifies the specific hazard of hub-and-spoke menus: the NPC seems unnaturally patient while the player works through every topic ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/analysis-conversation-design-in-games)). An NPC with real stakes should therefore be allowed to get impatient, refuse, or end the conversation. Putting these together gives a usable beat template, a synthesis rather than an industry standard. Open cold on the want with no greeting. Surface the obstacle or counter-want. Run two to four exchanges of tactic and counter-tactic, each shifting status or information. Turn on a reveal, refusal or concession. Exit on an unresolved question or a short player choice.

### Exposition has to arrive as ammunition

The phrase "exposition as ammunition" is McKee's, from *Story* (1997), not Blake Snyder's as it is sometimes credited. When a scene is thick with conflict, characters need every fact they can use against each other, and information flows into the action unnoticed. When conflict is missing, the writer falls back on *table dusting*, the classic maid-and-butler opening in which two servants chat purely to brief the audience ([Writing.Codidact quoting McKee](https://writing.codidact.com/posts/2104)). McKee's reported fix for two old friends reminiscing stiffly is to have one insult the other's immaturity. The audience still learns they go back years, and the scene gains friction ([NOWW](https://www.nowwwriters.ca/noww-blog/dialogue-shouldnt-say-it-out-loud)). Ingold's corresponding game rule is never to have characters explain what everyone in their world already knows, which is the "As you know, Bob" trap ([Robert Yang](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html)). Games also have a release valve that film lacks, and that is optional lore. Slabinski, crediting Jason Vandeberghe, warns against forcing lore on players. It belongs in side quests and codex entries the player opens by choice, and in main-path dialogue only when it is central. He praises Dragon Age for putting key explanations where they matter, which itself tells the player they matter ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Skolnick called front-loading a classic beginner mistake. Information should be seeded and dripped, and giving barely enough of it often increases curiosity ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-evan-skolnick-asks-game-writers-to-make-it-snappier-)).

The cleanest legitimate carrier for exposition is a character who genuinely does not know. In the Yakuza series, Kiryu's bafflement at modern slang works as a comic and expository device: the straight man stands in for the player ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/yakuza-6-the-song-of-lifes-localization-philosophy/)). God of War (2018) reportedly builds its exposition on a similar knowledge gap, with Atreus reading the Norse language Kratos cannot ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2018/01/12/god-of-war-ps4-boat.aspx)). Disco Elysium solves the problem of repeating critical information by having several different skill-voices raise it at key moments, which gives redundancy without anyone saying "as you know" ([RPG Codex on GameSpot](https://rpgcodex.net/article.php?id=11309)). Radio drama teaches the reverse lesson. When the audience cannot see, dialogue must carry what a viewer would have seen, but atmosphere is better carried by sound (an ice-cream van and birdsong for a heatwave) than by a character saying "it's hot" ([Pen to Print](https://pentoprint.org/friday-features-writing-audio-drama-a-brief-introduction-by-chris-gregory/)). A constructed before-and-after shows the technique. "As you know, the Ashen Guild has controlled the river trade for forty years since the Treaty of Varn" becomes "Forty years the Guild's bled this river. You want across? Pay them, or swim." The fact survives, attached to a threat and a want.

### Emotion lives in what characters refuse to say

Every craft source agrees that characters should not name their feelings. Nicklin tells writers not to have characters state their subtext, and to use stage directions to suggest the body language a real person would show ([Nicklin](https://gutefabrik.com/craft-writing-better-dialogue-a-2-day-workshop)). Ingold treats stating the theme aloud as a sign of weak writing ([Robert Yang](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html)). Games show the same principle at small scale. One Far Cry 2 enemy bark, discussed by Patrick Redding at GDC 2009, told the player three things at once: what the enemy was doing, that it had lost track of the player, and that it was afraid. It did all this without the enemy announcing fear ([Slabinski, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Slabinski's rewrite of his farmer turns a clinical job posting into a desperate appeal to the gods, conveying panic through register and urgency instead of an "I am desperate" line ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Pentiment, which has no voice acting, shows a character's changing standing in the protagonist's eyes by changing that character's font. Josh Sawyer said the team was glad to have avoided narrated asides about what the protagonist now thinks ([GameBanshee](https://gamebanshee.com/96xr8)). Behind all of these sits Hemingway's iceberg principle: material the writer knows and leaves out still strengthens what shows ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceberg_theory)). The techniques that follow from these sources form a practical toolkit, synthesized rather than taken from any single authority. A talkative character goes terse. Someone deflects into logistics ("Did you eat?"). Someone becomes oddly precise about a trivial detail. An action replaces a reply. A high-status character suddenly asks permission. Someone answers the feeling behind a question rather than the question. The pattern to forbid is the "I feel X because Y" sentence, along with characters summarizing their own arcs.

### Humor is a tactic, not a verbal tic

The most practical game-specific comedy advice comes from Ian Adams. Don't write strings of jokes, because jokes depend on delivery and pacing. Don't make every character a one-liner machine, because that flattens distinct voices. Get comedy from characters under pressure in situations that challenge their assumptions, since a funny situation produces the jokes for free. Cut a favorite joke if it slows play. Be willing not to be funny when stakes turn serious. Keep puns for meta text such as mission titles and item descriptions, because a character who keeps punning makes the world feel fake ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/6-tips-for-writing-video-game-dialogue-that-s-actually-funny)). He also puts a number on repetition: players tire of any line heard **more than three times an hour**, and a joke turns from smile to groan within about **two hours** of repetition ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/6-tips-for-writing-video-game-dialogue-that-s-actually-funny)). The cultural backlash against "Whedonspeak," the Marvel-style quip, centers on lines that serve the audience rather than the character and that deflate emotional moments ([Vice](https://creators.vice.com/en/article/v7deax/why-does-everyone-in-netflixs-cowboy-bebop-talk-like-that)). Forspoken became the game-industry example. Den of Geek traced its protagonist's snark to Whedon-style irony and found that detractors objected mostly to that modern register inside a fantasy world ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/games/forspokens-dialog-debate-controversy-examples)). The criticism is contested. The Daily Dot argued the backlash was disproportionate given how warmly audiences received Deadpool's snark ([Daily Dot](https://dailydot.com/?p=1289968)). The successes point in a consistent direction. Portal 2's comic leads began as gameplay functions (a guide, a tester), and Wheatley was written for Stephen Merchant's delivery ([Prima Games](https://primagames.com/news/portal-2-lets-make-caddyshack-1352805); [Destructoid](https://vip-develop.destructoid.com/?p=105268)). Yakuza's localizers balance absurdity against sincerity, with a straight man at the center ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/entertainment/video-games/sega-yakuza-games-second-life-west-1091255)). The criticized cases (Borderlands 3's target-less parody per [PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/borderlands-3-review/), Saints Row's uneven tone per [TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/reviews/saints-row)) failed on fit and control more than on being unfunny. That suggests a working test for any joke: would this character, in this emotional state, say it to this listener, and is it a tactic (deflecting pain, claiming status, defusing a threat) or a wink at the player?

### Brevity comes with numbers

Game writers cut harder than screenwriters because their lines compete with play. Slabinski's worked example takes a **62-word** quest pitch full of formal backstory and cuts it to **20 words** stating the threat, the task and the reward. He then rebuilds a 37-word version that adds character back in, and he sets a general target of cutting drafts to **a half or a third** ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Nicklin reminds writers that people rarely talk in paragraphs. Her exercises narrow the page to feel the on-screen limit and perform scripts aloud to expose stilted lines ([Nicklin](https://gutefabrik.com/craft-writing-better-dialogue-a-2-day-workshop)). The firmest numbers come from neighboring crafts. Comics editors cap balloons at around **25 words** and panels at roughly **35–50 words** ([Dark Horse script guide](https://images.darkhorse.com/darkhorse08/company/submissions/DHScriptFormatGuides.pdf); [Down the Tubes, Alan Moore](https://downthetubes.net/?p=6793)). Subtitle guidance caps a subtitle at **two lines of about 38 characters** ([80.lv, Ian Hamilton](https://80.lv/articles/10-golden-rules-on-subtitles-for-games)). One subtitle "card" therefore holds roughly 76 characters, or about 12–15 English words. That suggests a working budget, derived rather than published, of one to two cards (about 15–30 words) for most NPC lines, with longer speeches reserved for moments that earn them. Slabinski advises saving eloquence for climaxes, such as the speech before a final boss ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). For choices, Ingold recommends **about three options** per decision and Nicklin **one to five words** of choice text ([Robert Yang](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html); [Nicklin](https://gutefabrik.com/craft-writing-better-dialogue-a-2-day-workshop)). No source addressed contractions directly. Using them by default in speech, and reserving uncontracted speech as a marker for stiff, formal or alien characters, follows from the naturalism tests above but is an inference.

## Voice is engineered on paper before the first line is drafted

Studios decide who a character is before anyone writes their lines. Riot Games' account of creating Hwei, the League of Legends champion, is the most detailed public description. Voice work starts only once the broad backstory, personality and goals are settled. The narrative writer then produces four artifacts. First comes a *personality deck* of vocal references from comparable characters. Second is a *roster comparison* that names existing champions the new voice might overlap with (for Hwei: Jhin, Viego, Aphelios and Yone). Third is a set of sample lines, and fourth a casting sheet. Scripts then pass through four stages: a scratch script to explore the voice, an audition script covering the extremes of the character's emotional range, an exploratory script about half the final length written for the chosen actor, and a final script localized into **16 languages** ([Riot Games](https://www.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/dev/dev-the-voice-of-the-visionary/)). For Kindred, Riot settled the gameplay role first and built the character concept on top of it. Writer Matthew Dunn wrote roughly a dozen internal short stories, never shown to players, to test how the character fit the world, and modeled the pair's two halves on his own marriage ([Tribeca](https://tribecafilm.com/news/kindred-champion-riot-league-of-legends-charachter-design-mathew-dunn-riot-games)). God of War (2018) used the same technique. Cory Barlog wrote a short story about a hunting trip so the writers could feel the father-son dynamic before writing scenes ([PlayStation Blog](https://blog.playstation.com/2017/06/22/god-of-war-how-kratos-son-atreus-grew-from-concept-to-reality/)).

The "inner" fields on most character sheets come from older dramatic theory. Lajos Egri's *The Art of Dramatic Writing* (1946) divides a person into physiology (age, body, health, appearance), sociology (class, occupation, education, religion, politics, community standing) and psychology (morals, ambitions, frustrations, temperament). The writer must know all three, but they surface through behavior rather than statement ([Goodreads summary of Egri](https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/6058330-story-and-the-dimensions-of-character?tab=author)). K.M. Weiland's arc fields run as a chain. A *Wound* (Truby calls it the *Ghost*) produces a *Lie* the character believes. The *Want* is the conscious goal, and the *Need* is the truth that disproves the Lie, often reached only by giving up the Want ([Weiland](https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/character-arcs-3/)). McKee separates surface characterization from true character, which shows only in choices made under pressure ([Writers Store](https://writersstore.com/blogs/news/structure-and-character-excerpted-with-permission-from-the-book-story-part-one)). Games add fields that film templates lack. One is the feeling the player should have about the character: David Gaider wrote Dragon Age's Morrigan to talk about herself a lot while leaving players unsure how much was true, and he deliberately did not require her to be likeable ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrigan_(Dragon_Age))). Another is the place the character occupies. Chris Avellone builds minor NPCs from their location and the mood it should create, keeps backstory to a page or two, and gives companions opinions and a willingness to disagree, because players want someone to care about, not a yes-man ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/ku9rq); [GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/kahp)). Tabletop game-masters compress this further. They pick one or two defining traits and apply them consistently ([Syrinscape](https://blog.syrinscape.com/?p=1398)), keep each NPC's goal for the interaction in mind ([Sly Flourish on Matt Mercer](https://slyflourish.com/mercer_bringing_npcs_to_life.html)), and give every NPC a concrete, attainable want ([Rancourt](https://rancourt.substack.com/p/synthesizing-good-npc-advice)).

The most usable voice-differentiation tool on record is Stieg Hedlund's matrix for ChronoBlade. It gives each character a row with four independent axes. *Register* sits on a custom formality scale running from formal and grand through normal and colloquial to "insane." *Attitude* is the character's outlook. *Topics* are the subjects they keep returning to. *Quirks* are distinctive speech habits. Writers tried to satisfy as many axes as possible in each line while keeping lines short. Because the axes are independent, two characters can share a register and still sound different. The aim was for players to identify a speaker from a line alone, and before the matrix the team's results had been hit and miss ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/driving-character-voice-differences)). Nicklin's voice sheet adds texture: mannerisms, vocal tics, idioms, rhythms, pauses, interruptions, education and what she calls moments of poetry. She also gives the standard test, which is to cover the speaker names and check that you can still tell who is talking. For raw material she has students record and transcribe real conversation across ages and power relationships, and she avoids podcasts because their speakers perform ([Nicklin](https://gutefabrik.com/craft-writing-better-dialogue-a-2-day-workshop)). Casting adds actor-facing fields, and here concrete direction beats stacks of adjectives (see the production section). The table combines these sources into a voice profile an agent can fill in before drafting.

| Casting block | Age, voice type, range, accent (performed, not spelled), pronunciations | Riot casting sheet; VO briefs |
| Sample lines | Five to ten across neutral, angry, afraid, lying, tender, bark | Riot sample and audition lines |
| Arc deltas | What changes per act or relationship tier, and what never changes | Larian (BG3) |

Speech also depends on who is listening, and because forms of address are rarely symmetric, relationships are best mapped as ordered pairs (A speaking to B). Riot wrote an addendum for every line in Hwei's in-match story that plays when Jhin is in the same match ([Riot Games](https://www.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/dev/dev-the-voice-of-the-visionary/)). Kindred's two halves speaking to each other followed a separate set of rules from their lines to anyone else ([Tribeca](https://tribecafilm.com/news/kindred-champion-riot-league-of-legends-charachter-design-mathew-dunn-riot-games)). Gaider has described party banter as a way for players to see their own choices argued over by companions ([Fanlore summarizing Gaider](https://fanlore.org/wiki/Party_banter)). Over the course of a game, voices have to change without becoming unrecognizable. Larian's writing director Adam Smith said Baldur's Gate 3's companions kept the heart of their original concepts while everything else stayed open to change ([Yahoo/BAFTA report](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/create-cast-baldurs-gate-3-211403155.html)). Riot tied Hwei's in-match micro-arcs to gameplay milestones such as ranking up his ultimate ability ([Riot Games](https://www.leagueoflegends.com/en-us/news/dev/dev-the-voice-of-the-visionary/)). A practical consequence is to tag each profile field as core (never changes) or arc-variable, and to check late-game lines against the core fields alone. Each voice change should also be earned by a choice made under pressure on screen, as McKee's model implies.

### Dialect, period speech and research mostly stay below the waterline

The consensus on dialect is to apply it lightly. Elmore Leonard's seventh rule is to use regional dialect sparingly, because once the page fills with phonetic spellings and apostrophes there is no stopping ([Brain Pickings](https://www.brainpickings.org/blog/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/)). Heavy phonetic rendering forces readers to decode instead of follow ([MasterClass](https://www.masterclass.com/articles/how-to-write-character-accents)), and it can carry prejudice against the people being depicted ([LitReactor](https://litreactor.com/columns/do-or-dialect-6-tips-for-building-a-believable-voice/)). Word choice and syntax should carry regionality instead. For fantasy and period settings, Slabinski warns against defaulting to "thee and thou" archaism. He notes that invented expletives, like *Battlestar Galactica*'s "frak," set a world apart, and that much of the felt authenticity comes from the actor's conviction ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Hedlund's team used archaic wording only as one character's quirk, not as everyone's default ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/driving-character-voice-differences)). Research follows Egri's know-but-don't-state principle. Riot's Dunn read widely on how cultures perceive death to build a death-themed champion ([Tribeca](https://tribecafilm.com/news/kindred-champion-riot-league-of-legends-charachter-design-mathew-dunn-riot-games)). Sucker Punch relied on reference histories and had Sony's Japan team confirm that every kanji shown in Ghost of Tsushima existed in the 13th century ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/sucker-punch-discusses-ghost-of-tsushimas-approach-to-cultural-authenticity)). The practical rule is to collect far more jargon, idiom and period vocabulary than will ever appear. Use only the terms a player can decode from context, record pronunciations for voice actors, and in voiced games leave accent to a cast performer rather than spelling it on the page. Phonetic respelling also hurts localization and screen readers.

### Authenticity and canon need humans in the loop

Studios that write outside their own experience describe consulting early and continuously rather than checking at the end. Sucker Punch producer Brian Fleming described the team's stance as being a learner. Sony's Japan team reviewed Ghost of Tsushima from the original pitch onward, including every video ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/sucker-punch-discusses-ghost-of-tsushimas-approach-to-cultural-authenticity)). Critics still argued the game slipped into stereotype in places ([Past Meets Pixel](https://pastmeetspixel.substack.com/p/ghost-of-tsushima-visual-historiography)). For Tell Me Why, GLAAD's Nick Adams worked with Dontnod for **more than two years**, from story foundations through casting and script review at every phase, and trans actor August Black contributed significantly to his character's dialogue ([Tell Me Why FAQ](https://tellmewhygame.com/faq); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/feature/nbc-out/tell-me-why-video-game-features-transgender-lead-character-n1239123)). AbleGamers' disability sensitivity-reading service pairs two readers who review scripts and watch a gameplay slice, with a turnaround of about ten days ([AbleGamers](https://accessible.games/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/AbleGamers-Sensitivity-Reading-OneSheet.pdf)). Some critics regard sensitivity readers as a form of censorship ([TechXplore](https://techxplore.com/news/2023-12-bad-video-games-learnt-embrace.pdf)). An agent cannot settle that debate, but it can follow the documented practice. It should flag characters and lines that need consultation and never certify its own authenticity.

Canon works the same way. Ubisoft's Anna Megill argues that a story bible must be a living document that guides every discipline and leaves room to grow, not a static file updated only in emergencies ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/building-a-basic-story-bible-for-your-game)). In 2018 World of Warcraft had **three lore historians** who answered research requests, reviewed content and sat in story rooms. Their research occasionally forced planned stories to change ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/2018/08/14/a-look-inside-how-blizzard-maintains-world-of-warcrafts-lore)), as when writers had planned a storyline for a character who was already dead ([Wowhead](https://es.wowhead.com/news/game-informer-interview-on-wows-lore-and-story-consistency-286451)). Community sources describe the historians as recording what Blizzard decides is canon rather than deciding it ([Warcraft Wiki](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Loreology)). That is a sensible model for an agent: check every proper noun and timeline fact against the bible, and list any new facts its lines introduce as proposed canon for human approval. Handoffs show why the voice profile matters. Gaider planned to write Dragon Age II's Merrill, handed her to Mary Kirby, who at first struggled to find the voice, and said his own version would have been far more serious ([GameBanshee](https://gamebanshee.com/cfp4)). The profile and its sample lines are the handoff artifact that keeps a voice stable across writers, or across agent sessions.

## Structure decides what a player's choice means

Branching dialogue has a fundamental economic problem. Every choice that leads to unique content commits the writer to more content further down, so a naive tree grows exponentially. Sam Kabo Ashwell's 2015 catalogue of standard patterns remains the shared vocabulary for how working designs avoid that growth ([Ashwell](https://heterogenoustasks.wordpress.com/2015/01/26/standard-patterns-in-choice-based-games/)). Ashwell stresses that real works mix these patterns.

| Pattern | Shape | What it buys | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Time Cave | Heavy branching, little rejoining | No state tracking, maximal freedom | Most content unseen; many endings; freedom the player can't comprehend |
| Gauntlet | Linear spine; side branches die, backtrack or rejoin quickly | Cheap; most players see most content | The constraint is visible |
| Branch and Bottleneck | Branches rejoin at shared events; state carries the difference | Personality shaping with a manageable plot | Needs state; early playthroughs feel alike |
| Quest | Modular clusters rejoining toward a few endings | Suits journeys and geography | Can feel fragmentary |
| Open Map | Quest with reversible travel | Leisurely exploration | Slower pacing; heavy state |
| Sorting Hat | Early choices route the player into one of several linear branches | Breadth plus depth | Effectively writing several games |
| Floating Modules | No trunk; encounters unlocked by state or chance | Modular, multi-author | Few assumptions possible; needs volume |
| Loop and Grow / Spoke and Hub | Story loops to a center; state opens new options each pass | Replay with in-fiction logic | Needs a reason for the repetition |

Emily Short's "Beyond Branching" adds structures that abandon the tree altogether. In *quality-based narrative*, pioneered by Fallen London, *storylets* unlock when numeric qualities meet their conditions. A three-clue murder needs four storylets, one gated on the other three, instead of a tree of every order in which the clues might be found. *Salience-based* systems pick from a pool the most specific line whose tags match the current world state, falling back to defaults. *Waypoint* narrative attaches lines to transitions between topics and lets the NPC steer toward plot points ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)). Short describes a storylet as content plus prerequisites plus effects: simple, atomic and recombinable. She prototypes storylets on index cards ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2019/11/29/storylets-you-want-them/)), warns that teaching only node-and-link tools like Twine pushes authors toward combinatorial explosion, and recommends testing such systems with thousands of random playthroughs to find dead or overused content ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)). Choice of Games' house rules turn branch-and-bottleneck into discipline. Prefer *delayed branching*. Make every choice modify at least one stat, and have every chapter both change stats and test them. Reference specific past choices only sparingly. Avoid over-merging, which strips the work of its interactivity ([Choice of Games](https://www.choiceofgames.com/2011/07/by-the-numbers-how-to-write-a-long-interactive-novel-that-doesnt-suck/)). A rule follows from Ashwell's point that branch-and-bottleneck without state collapses into a gauntlet: every foldback must leave a flag or stat behind, or the branch was cosmetic. Matching structure to job, a single NPC conversation that must reach a fixed beat suits a hub written as branch-then-rejoin. Personality-shaping arcs suit branch-and-bottleneck with stats. Exploration games suit quest or open-map designs with storylets. Replayable roguelites and ambient chatter suit salience pools.

### Three options, each a different move

At the scale of a single conversation, Ingold's AdventureX talk gives the most concrete design rules. Offer **about three options**, because two reads as a right-or-wrong binary and four loses focus. Make the three a different *move* rather than a different tone: *Accept* cooperates with the current topic, *Reject* pushes back or attacks, *Deflect* changes the subject or escapes, ideally without undercutting the moment with a joke. Never include filler options that exist only to give the player something to click. Use *loops* for conversations that circle at one intensity, add a *trapdoor* option that exits the loop, and let a Reject *reset* the loop to its start to show how hard the NPC pushes back. NPCs must respond to what the player actually said, and players must always be able to tell where they are in a conversation ([Robert Yang](https://www.blog.radiator.debacle.us/2018/11/notes-on-sparking-dialogue-great.html); [Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/developer-shows-how-to-write-good-game-dialogue-using-b-1830797912)). He compressed the philosophy into one line: "Branching is action, action is character, character is drama." Accept, Reject and Deflect map naturally onto improv's accept-or-block logic and onto Johnstone's status moves, which is an inference but a useful one. The scheme also explains why the familiar good/neutral/evil triad performs badly. Critics of BioWare's dialogue wheel argue that fixed tone positions teach players to pick by color or slot rather than by content ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/mass-effect-dialogue-wheels-are-flawed/)), whereas options that differ in what is actually done (ask, comply, lie, bargain, leave) force a real decision.

### Promised consequences must pay off

Telltale's *The Walking Dead* made the on-screen notice that a character will remember your choice famous, and then infamous. Den of Geek's flowchart analysis found Season One largely linear, with only one episode splitting into distinct paths ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/games/the-walking-dead-false-promises-telltale/)). Game Informer pointed out that saving either of two characters early on ends with the same death ([Game Informer](https://www.gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2015/02/03/why-your-choices-dont-matter-in-telltale-games.aspx)). A dissenting academic reading argues that Telltale's choices work as moral thought experiments rather than plot switches, so judging them by branching misses their function ([Eludamos](https://eludamos.org/index.php/eludamos/article/view/vol9no1-2)). Critics praised Kentucky Route Zero for offering expressive choices without the portentous suggestion that they would change the plot ([Critical Distance](https://critical-distance.com/2019/09/26/kentucky-route-zero/)). Taken together, the position this evidence supports is that expressive, flavor-only choices are legitimate, provided the game never claims they are more than that. If the game signals that a choice is remembered, at least one later line must react to it. Budgets enforce this discipline whether writers like it or not, since every extra line and cutscene costs money ([The Mancunion](https://mancunion.com/?p=35670)).

### State turns memory into lines

Reactivity rests on a handful of mechanisms. Flags and stats are set by choices and tested later. Visit counts hide topics the player has exhausted. Relationship values gate how easily an NPC agrees. Skill checks gate options. Ink builds several of these into the language: every knot and stitch automatically counts its visits, once-only choices (`*`) disappear after use, sticky choices (`+`) persist for exits and "ask about something else" options, and a text-less fallback choice fires when everything else is exhausted ([ink documentation](https://github.com/inkle/ink/blob/master/Documentation/WritingWithInk.md)). Baldur's Gate 3 shows relationship values changing the *cost* of persuasion rather than locking content away. Writer Kevin VanOrd explained that a companion with high enough trust follows the player after a single word, while one with less trust has to be persuaded with a roll. He also admitted the team could not make every character react to every situation ([GameGeeker summarizing Edge](https://gamegeeker.com/de/news/baldurs-gate-3-companion-affinity-system-interview?market=DE)). BG3 shows the dice on screen and treats failure as content rather than punishment ([CGMagazine](https://www.cgmagonline.com/articles/baldurs-gate-3-redefined-crpg-dialogue/)). The mechanic still divided players enough that Larian added a "loaded dice" option ([Bell of Lost Souls](https://www.belloflostsouls.net/?p=366169)). Disco Elysium separates passive checks, which fire automatically when a skill is high enough and offer extra insight, from active rolls, and its critics found failure often more interesting than success ([Game Critics](https://gamecritics.com/mike-suskie/disco-elysium-review/)). The cheapest visible reactivity, a synthesis rather than a sourced rule, comes from varying a scene's *entry* (the greeting reflects state) and *exit* (the farewell reflects what just happened). Checks should be written so that failure yields something worth having, such as new information, a complication or a joke, and the writer should decide up front whether a check can be retried.

### Systemic dialogue picks the most specific true line

For large volumes of contextual speech, such as companion chatter, combat lines and roguelite reactions, studios use rule databases instead of trees. Elan Ruskin's GDC 2012 talk described Valve's system, used from Left 4 Dead onward. It fuzzy-matches **hundreds of facts** about the world against **thousands of candidate lines**, picks the most specific match and falls back to more general rules. It tracks facts through a single mechanism so characters remember history, and it lets writers add special cases and running gags without programmer help ([GDC Vault](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1015528/AI-driven-Dynamic-Dialog-through)). Short's recap stresses its design goals: situation-specific quips (being attacked in the circus versus anywhere else) and conversations that break off sensibly when a speaker comes under attack ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2012/03/16/gdc-2012-talk-on-dynamic-dialogue/)). She reports that Firewatch later used a Ruskin-style system ([Emily Short](https://emshort.blog/2016/04/12/beyond-branching-quality-based-and-salience-based-narrative-structures/)). Hades pushed the approach to tens of thousands of voiced lines. Greg Kasavin described the method as inventing as many contexts as possible in which a character might speak, loading them all, and letting the game choose. Content was written to occur in any order, so a meeting with Aphrodite plays differently depending on whether the player has already met Athena that run ([GeekDad](https://geekdad.com/2019/10/narrative-and-early-access-supergiants-greg-kasavin-discusses-hades-development/)). Zagreus alone has about **8,500 lines** ([But Why Tho](https://butwhytho.net/2021/08/13/interview-developing-hades-with-supergiant-games-greg-kasavin/)). Writing for such systems calls for a discipline drawn from these sources. Write the generic fallback for each trigger first, then add specificity in layers (character, location, recent event, relationship, repetition count). Make every line order-independent, or state any dependency as an explicit condition. Have each line write a memory fact when it plays, so it never repeats and running gags can escalate. Give story-critical lines priority over ambient chatter.

### Real-time conversations must survive being cut off

Walk-and-talk dialogue adds a timing problem. Night School's Oxenfree lets players keep moving while speech bubbles appear above the protagonist's head. The player can answer, interrupt or stay silent, and options fade as the moment passes ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxenfree)). Co-founder Sean Krankel said the interrupt feature meant a great deal of extra work, and the nonlinear script ran to **about 1,500 pages** ([MCV/Develop](https://mcvuk.com/?p=180442)). Reviewers' main complaint was ambiguity: it was unclear whether a chosen line would cut the speaker off immediately or wait for a lull ([Quarter Disorder](https://quarterdisorder.com/2016/01/23/review-oxenfree/)). For the sequel, the developers made silence an explicit, valid choice ([Bloody Disgusting](https://bloody-disgusting.com/video-games/3768039/latest-oxenfree-ii-lost-signal-developer-video-explores-choice-video/)). God of War (2018) gave long stories a safe stage in its boat rides, where conversation would not be lost to combat ([Vice](https://vice.com/en/article/god-of-war-smartly-fixes-the-very-bad-way-most-games-handle-dialogue)). Its sequel shows what goes wrong when that machinery slips: Ragnarök's day-one patch fixed stories being interrupted incorrectly and dialogue playing out of order ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/god-of-war-ragnaroks-day-1-patch-includes-over-160-fixes/)). The writing rules that follow are inferences from these cases. Split conversations into self-contained beats of one or two lines, each a safe place to cut. Give each beat a resume rule (restart, skip or drop). Write short pickup lines that bridge back after an interruption, and cut-off tails ending in a dash. Put critical information early in a beat or repeat it in the pickup. Gate long conversations on calm states such as traversal or riding in a vehicle. Expire stale lines rather than playing them late. Tell the player whether a choice interrupts or waits.

### Outline before drafting

Professionals map branches before polishing lines. The Witcher 3's lead writer Jakub Szamałek described tracking every branch on paper with tables, trees, diagrams and sticky notes, and called it extremely difficult ([Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2019/10/the_witcher_3s_writing_team_was_worried_the_game_didnt_have_enough_content)). Larian's lead writer Chrystal Ding described a huge spreadsheet tracking outcome paths ([Yahoo/PC Gamer](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/larian-gave-baldurs-gate-3-022539159.html)). A workflow assembled from these practices (a synthesis) has five steps. Write a one-paragraph scene brief covering purpose, required beats, what the NPC wants, what the player might want, and what state is read and written. Choose a structure. Sketch the graph or beat sheet. Write the default path first and the variants second. Finish with a reachability pass checking that every node can be reached, every flag is both set and read, no topic repeats, and a fallback exists.

## The player's own lines are the most constrained in the game

How defined the protagonist is determines how their lines can be written. The Witcher 3 gives Geralt short option labels that he expands into fuller, in-character speech. One reviewer felt CD Projekt Red had abandoned even the pretense of a blank slate and nudged Geralt along a path rather than handing him over ([GameBanshee](http://www.gamebanshee.com/kaaqq)). Mass Effect's Shepard was designed to be not entirely blank. Actor Mark Meer was directed to play a restrained professional so that switching between the heroic Paragon and ruthless Renegade lines would not feel like two different people ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_Shepard)). Disco Elysium splits the protagonist into **24 skills** that speak like secondary characters, and Robert Kurvitz's stated goal was that players shouldn't notice they were reading ([Edge via PressReader](https://www.pressreader.com/australia/edge/20181206/283338998326196)). The skills comment, bicker and open new options when passive checks succeed ([Game Critics](https://gamecritics.com/mike-suskie/disco-elysium-review/)). Baldur's Gate 3's writing director put the player at the center: the main character is the person playing, whether through an origin character or a custom one ([Yahoo/PC Gamer](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/larian-gave-baldurs-gate-3-022539159.html)). For silent protagonists, Ryan Matejka warns against NPCs explaining the situation and the next steps unprompted. He recommends giving the personality to supporting characters and the environment, embracing ambiguity, and avoiding forced options that misrepresent what the player intends ([How To Write a Game](https://howtowriteagame.substack.com/p/how-to-write-a-video-game-with-a)). One working technique, inferred rather than sourced, is to have the NPC's next line react to an implied player beat ("No need to look at me like that") instead of delivering a monologue.

| PC model | Example | How options work | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| Defined and voiced | Geralt | Short label expands into an in-character line | Label and delivery drift apart |
| Semi-defined and voiced | Shepard | Tone-positioned paraphrase on a wheel | Choosing by color, not content |
| Player-authored text | Choice of Games, classic CRPGs | Full first-person text is the line | Gendered or assumed self-reference |
| Internal chorus | Disco Elysium | Skill voices interject and unlock options | Volume of text |
| Silent or vessel | Gordon Freeman | Actions or attitudes; NPCs react to implied beats | NPC monologues that explain everything |

The voiced protagonist creates the *paraphrase problem*. Drew Karpyshyn explained that once Shepard was fully voiced, showing the exact line and then hearing it spoken felt redundant, so the wheel shows a short summary ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k44c8)). Tom Francis observed that the gap between label and line is sometimes dramatic, and that options producing essentially the same line make the choice feel meaningless ([Pentadact](https://www.pentadact.com/?p=423)). Fallout 4 became the case study. TechRaptor argues the problem was never the four-option limit but labels that didn't map sensibly onto what the voiced protagonist then said. A label like "kill him" could mean several things, and two or three of the four options often led down the same path ([TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/fallout-4s-dialogue-wheel-short-answers-make-no-sense)). Ink offers a structural fix. Square brackets let text before the bracket appear in both the option and the output, text inside appear only in the option, and text after appear only in the output, so the option can be the literal opening of the spoken line ([ink documentation](https://github.com/inkle/ink/blob/master/Documentation/WritingWithInk.md)). Combining the critiques gives a set of label rules. The label must let the player predict both the intent and the tone of the line. The delivered line must never add a threat, lie, commitment or reveal the label didn't signal. Voiced labels should compress faithfully, as a verb plus an attitude ("Threaten him, quietly") rather than a cryptic keyword. No two options may resolve to the same line unless the response or the state change differs. Silence deserves a slot of its own. Firewatch lets the player not answer Delilah at all ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firewatch)), and the Oxenfree sequel treats silence as a deliberate choice.

### A customizable protagonist needs neutral address and whole-line variants

When the player defines the protagonist, NPCs face a practical problem: what do they call this person? Shipped games converge on a few answers. Mass Effect gave its custom hero **a fixed surname** so voiced characters could address them naturally ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_Shepard)). Cyberpunk 2077 fixed the name itself, and before launch CD Projekt's Max Pears said NPCs would address the player as "V" rather than with gendered terms ([GameRevolution](https://www.gamerevolution.com/?p=595629)). Post-launch reporting says gendered address follows the chosen voice, which suggests "V" in most lines and voice-keyed gendered terms elsewhere ([PCGamesN](https://www.pcgamesn.com/cyberpunk-2077/character-creator)). The cost of speaking typed names aloud is visible in Fallout 4: its robot butler can say **1,259 pre-recorded names** in English ([Guinness World Records](https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/world-records/457631-most-names-spoken-by-a-robot-in-a-videogame)). Starfield lets players choose he, she or they, and Todd Howard said Bethesda recorded all the relevant dialogue to support each ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/starfield-pronouns-character-creation-reddit-ama-todd-howard/)). Dragon Age: The Veilguard sets pronouns separately from gender identity ([WBUR](https://www.wbur.org/hereandnow/2024/10/30/dragon-age-the-veilguard)). Failbetter replaced the gender question with a *preferred form of address*. It dropped a comic stammer that had greeted its third gender option after mixed feedback, and it added neutral titles, some unlocked by profession ([Failbetter Games](https://www.failbettergames.com/the-mysterious-and-indistinct-art-of-pronouns/)). English has no widely known neutral equivalent of sir or madam, so the guidance is to acknowledge rank or politeness some other way ([Representme](https://representme.charity/upload/Supplement%20_%20Pronouns%20and%20gender%20inclusive%20language.pdf)). Fallbacks need testing. One player reported a non-binary character being called "Sir" in nearly every line because "Sir" was the default branch of a two-way switch ([itch.io](https://itch.io/post/11831116)). Identity reactivity should sit on top of a shared spine. Dragon Age: Origins changes how characters treat the hero according to race and origin while every class follows the same main plot ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dragon_Age:_Origins)), and BG3 extends class reactivity even after a respec ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/baldurs-gate-3-final-qa-larian-on-origin-vs-custom-characters-rule-changes-crossplay-and-more/amp/)).

Gender is far harder in translation than in English. Localization lead Mike Souto calls speaker name, speaker gender and recipient gender the minimum translators need, because male-to-male, male-to-female, female-to-male and female-to-female exchanges can be very different strings ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/developers-make-your-game-localization-friendly-a-few-tips-part-2-)). A Brazilian Portuguese localizer strongly advises against swapping single gendered words through variables, because noun and adjective agreement breaks. The preferred approach is to mark every gendered sentence and create a full copy per gender, including neopronoun forms ([Whatever the Wind Brings](https://whateverthewindbrings.bearblog.dev/player-gender-in-video-game-localization/)). Netflix's games internationalization requirements recommend phrasing that minimizes gender agreement, such as preferring nouns to adjectives ([Netflix Backlot](https://backlothelp.netflix.com/hc/en-us/articles/4411631891859-Netflix-Games-Internationalization-Requirements)). The resulting default rules are synthesized from these sources. Address the protagonist in the second person, by a title, role or epithet earned through deeds, or by a fixed name or callsign. Never put the typed name in voiced lines. Avoid sir, madam, lad and lass unless they sit inside an explicit variant. Write first-person options without gendered self-reference unless the option is gated on the matching flag. Annotate speaker and addressee gender on every line, even when the English is neutral. A neutral English line such as "You must be exhausted" still needs *épuisé* or *épuisée* in French.

## Frequency, not content, sets the rules for each line type

A line's type depends mostly on what fires it and how often it repeats. Systemic lines are chosen at runtime from world state, and Valve's dialogue system and the context-aware NPC dialogue Naughty Dog presented at GDC 2014 both work this way ([GDC Vault, Valve](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1015528/AI-driven-Dynamic-Dialog-through); [GDC Vault, Naughty Dog](https://www.gdcvault.com/play/1020386/A-Context-Aware-Character-Dialog)). Systemic lines need many variants and a single job each, and they have to be tested in context. Authored lines play once and can afford subtext and length. Clint Hocking drew the sharpest distinction. Cutscene dialogue should avoid stating what is visually obvious, but barks are feedback about a change in game state, so they may state the obvious and need no reaction shot ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/why-video-game-characters-say-such-ridiculous-things-5921878)). Before writing any line, the writer needs to know five things: its trigger, its expected frequency, its speaker, what the player can see at that moment, and whether it can be interrupted. The table summarizes conventions by type. Sourcing is strong for barks, hints and narrators and thin for vendors, achievements, loading tips and diegetic signage, where the rules below are mostly inference.

| Line type | Trigger and frequency | Form | Governing rule | Signature failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Combat / AI-state bark | Engine event; very high | 1–6 words, near-imperative | One fact the player can act on; honest to real AI state | Clever lines that turn vague or grating |
| Reaction / idle bark | Player action or proximity; high | One short sentence | Distinctiveness inversely proportional to frequency | Arrow in the knee |
| Ambient / overheard chatter | Area entry; high | Fragments of everyday life, sliced mid-conversation | Subtle; rumors must point to real content | Pointing players at wrong goals |
| Companion banter / interjection | Location, party, story flags; medium | Resumable beats of 2–4 lines | Opinions on each other and on the player's choices | Silent trigger bugs; lost info when cut off |
| Quest-giver dialogue | Scripted; once | Characterful but states the ask once plainly | Want first, task clear, hook last | Lore dump before the ask |
| Objective / journal text | Every quest-state change | Imperative verb plus target; journal in voice | Player can always recover what to do next | Objective drifts from observable state |
| Hint / nag | Time stuck plus evidence; variable | Escalating: notice, then mechanic, then solution | Delay, escalate, cap, offer a setting | Solving the puzzle aloud in seconds |
| Narrator / death / retry | Systemic events; very high in roguelikes | Past tense, reactive | Never interrupt play; spend variants where repetition is highest | Repeating across runs |
| PC self-talk | Exploration and puzzles | Rare observations, not solutions | Speaks *for* the player, so ration it | Stash and puzzle muttering |
| Audio log / letter | Found; once, any order | A scene with a stake, cut off at the end | Self-contained, a confessional | Moving core plot into optional logs |
| Codex / item text | Opened by the player | Function first, then one oblique lore fact | Scannable, skippable, accurate | Lore the player is forced to read |
| Efforts | Animation events; very high | Grunts, gasps, deaths by intensity | Recorded last, vocally stressful | (No craft source found) |
| Vendor / greeting | Proximity or menu; very high | Pools by state: first meeting, repeat, broke, farewell | Blandest pool where frequency is highest | Memorable greeting heard hourly |

### Barks: one fact, many variants, blandest where most frequent

Ubisoft defines barks as phrases or sounds NPCs make during a triggered event ([Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7Cm07zbBGy4Xml6WgYi25d/the-convergence-of-ai-and-creativity-introducing-ghostwriter)). Richard Dansky, who wrote for the Rainbow Six and Splinter Cell series, says their main job is to deliver gameplay information (flanking, grenade, I'm hit). Each bark should carry **a single piece of information**, because every combination of facts needs its own set of variants. The most common barks are heard most often, so they have to be the best ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/why-video-game-characters-say-such-ridiculous-things-5921878)). The Far Cry 2 bark that conveyed three facts at once does not contradict this. It reported one state change (the enemy lost the player) and layered awareness and fear into it by implication instead of stringing separate facts together. Nels Anderson of Mark of the Ninja warns that clever barks turn vague or stand out. His cautionary example is a guard who dismissed a disturbance as his imagination while staring at a light the player had visibly smashed, because the bark was keyed to the sound event rather than to what the guard knew ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/why-video-game-characters-say-such-ridiculous-things-5921878)). Chris Dahlen compared writing barks to naming individual grains of rice in a burrito, and found that a dozen ways of saying one idea was much harder than expected ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/why-video-game-characters-say-such-ridiculous-things-5921878)). Practitioners overgenerate on purpose. Slabinski writes about **100 variations to find the best 5–10** ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/8-key-principles-of-writing-effective-game-dialogue)). Matejka requires every bark to respond to a trigger, give information and reflect the speaker. He also warns that a line true to the character can still feel like the developer mocking the player, such as a sidekick remarking that you are lost while you backtrack. The fix is to soften the line or give it to a different speaker ([How To Write a Game](https://howtowriteagame.substack.com/p/how-to-write-video-game-barks)). Skyrim supplied the canonical warning. Emil Pagliarulo wrote the arrow-in-the-knee guard line late at night while churning through guard lines, and the Radiant AI system played it so often near the player that it became the defining meme of late 2011 ([Bethesda](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-EU/news/how-an-arrow-in-the-knee-made-its-way-to-skyrim)). The lesson is to make lines bland and functional on common triggers and save quirky lines for rare ones. Barks can also make violence mean something. The Last of Us Part II names every human enemy and has squadmates call out to each other and react when one falls ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/2020/05/27/a-spoiler-free-overview-of-the-last-of-us-part-ii)). Far Cry 2 recorded some barks in Afrikaans and Swahili, which added atmosphere and hid repetition from players who don't speak those languages ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/why-video-game-characters-say-such-ridiculous-things-5921878)). No published standard exists for variants per trigger or for cooldown times. The defensible heuristic is to size each pool so the same line rarely recurs within a play session.

### Ambient chatter and companion banter build the world without demanding attention

Natalie Mikkelson's taxonomy of ambient lines covers thinking aloud, overheard banter (lines lifted from the middle or end of a longer conversation), everyday life, contrasting personalities in the same job, rumors as "carrots," reactions to the player, and gameplay aids. Her method is to list the verbs an NPC is performing and write from those. Her catalogue of mistakes includes full conversations for every NPC, identical voices, over-the-top quirks (ambient lines should be as subtle as possible because they repeat), pointing players at wrong goals, and projecting modern values onto invented societies ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/adding-life-to-worlds-with-dialogue-barks)). Ken Levine's rule for BioShock was that every AI should tell a story ([GameBanshee](https://gamebanshee.com/fh4c)). Companion banter is a system of reactions. Gaider describes each companion responding to player choices in character, so players learn what input produces what reaction ([Kill Screen](https://www.killscreen.com/interview-david-gaider/)). The scale is enormous: Gaider's team wrote roughly **1.2 million words** for Baldur's Gate II ([GameSpot](https://gamespot.com/articles/dragon-age-writer-shares-stories-and-more-from-his/1100-6470485/)). Banter also fails silently. Swen Vincke said about **1,500 lines** for one BG3 companion weren't triggering because of a bug ([Bell of Lost Souls](https://www.belloflostsouls.net/2023/08/baldurs-gate-3-more-than-1500-lines-of-dialogue-to-be-restored-in-patch-2.html)). Reviewers praised Uncharted 4 for vehicle conversations that resume naturally after the player gets out and comes back ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/article/15644-uncharted-4-is-filled-with-stupidly-impressive-details)). Neil Druckmann limited that game's dialogue choices to a few spots where an option added something ([GamesRadar](https://gamesradar.com/uncharted-4-dialogue-options)). Banter should therefore be written as resumable beats with explicit trigger conditions that QA can test. Critical information should never sit in a beat that can be cut off.

### Quest text, hints and narrators carry gameplay weight in a character's voice

Quest text has one job above characterization: the player must always be able to work out what to do next. Tool documentation gives each objective a short field such as "Find the key" and marks side objectives optional ([TaleNode](https://talenode.readthedocs.io/en/latest/user-guide/quests/)). Practitioners recommend a shared quest state machine whose triggers have observable cues ([PulseGeek](https://pulsegeek.com/articles/quest-scripting-checklist-for-writers/)). A three-layer convention follows, synthesized rather than formally sourced. The objective line is an imperative verb plus a target, under about eight words, and updates on every state change. The journal entry recaps in a voice why the quest matters. The quest-giver's dialogue can be characterful but states the core ask once in plain words.

Hints are the most criticized line type when their timing is wrong. Shigeru Miyamoto said in 1999 that Navi's advice system was Ocarina of Time's biggest weak point. He had wanted to remove it but judged that worse for players, and he kept Navi deliberately simple-minded because smarter hints would have made her failures more conspicuous ([Destructoid](https://www.destructoid.com/old-interview-highlights-miyamotos-misgivings-with-navi-in-ocarina-of-time/)). Eiji Aonuma defended her as relief from lonely adventuring ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navi_(The_Legend_of_Zelda))). God of War Ragnarök's companions offered puzzle solutions almost immediately. The narrative director said playtests had never produced complaints about characters talking too much, and acknowledged that the first-hint timer was too aggressive ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/god-of-war-ragnarok-devs-unaware-chatty-companions-until-launch/)). The PC port added a setting to reduce companion hint frequency ([Wccftech](https://wccftech.com/god-of-war-ragnarok-to-let-pc-players-reduce-intrusive-puzzle-hints/amp/)). Horizon Forbidden West's patch 1.06 cut how often Aloy mentions her stash, a trigger coverage said could fire tens or hundreds of times per playthrough ([The Loadout](https://www.theloadout.com/horizon-forbidden-west/patch-106-stash-mentions)). Critics also objected to Aloy muttering solutions before players had looked around ([GamesRadar](https://www.gamesradar.com/horizon-forbidden-west-is-the-latest-aaa-game-to-realize-players-arent-idiots-who-immediately-need-puzzles-explained-to-them/)). No source published actual hint timings. A defensible hint ladder, inferred from these failures, runs from silence and environmental cues, to an observation naming the object of interest, to naming the mechanic, to a near-solution. Each step is gated on elapsed time *and* evidence of being stuck, such as failed attempts or wandering. Nag lines get hard caps, and players get a frequency setting. Who speaks the hint matters. A companion's hint can read as the companion's own curiosity, while protagonist self-talk reads as the game doing the player's thinking.

Narrators turn systemic events into story. Kasavin said Bastion's reactive narration solved a design problem: a game that never interrupted play for its story and still carried emotional weight ([Game Informer](https://gameinformer.com/b/features/archive/2011/09/09/bastion-afterwords.aspx)). He reportedly cut hundreds of narration lines that didn't enhance play, avoided repeating lines unless the player repeated content, and credited Logan Cunningham's voice as the reason the technique was attempted at all ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bastion_(video_game))). Chris Bourassa wrote Darkest Dungeon's narration to Wayne June's cadence, aiming for Gothic without camp ([GameSpot](https://www.gamespot.com/articles/the-gothic-sensibilities-of-darkest-dungeon/1100-6424880/)). In roguelikes, death and retry lines are the most frequently heard narrative lines. That is where the variant budget belongs, alongside a priority system that prefers unheard, state-specific lines. Hades reportedly limits each character to one conversation per return visit ([Inven Global](https://www.invenglobal.com/datalab/articles/14783/supergiant-games-how-indie-developers-made-goty-hades)), and its players still complain that some characters repeat ([Steam discussion](https://steamcommunity.com/app/1145360/discussions/0/2987539784417139179)).

### Documents, flavor text and boxes reward economy

Ken Levine calls audio logs *confessionals*, a direct line into a character's private voice ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/bioshock-infinite-interview-ken-levine)). He uses diaries to tell players about parts of the city they haven't reached yet ([GameBanshee](https://gamebanshee.com/fh4c)). One critic argued that BioShock Infinite leaned on audio logs too heavily for core story ([Gameranx](https://gameranx.com/features/id/13983/article/phil-s-story-corner-bioshock-infinite-s-overreliance-on-audio-logs-is-bad-storytelling/)). The working inference is that a log should be a scene rather than a summary, with one speaker, a specific moment and a stake, that makes sense heard alone and in any order. In Souls-like games, item descriptions carry much of the story through short, fragmentary glimpses of a lost world ([Walantus, GDC 2017 Narrative Review](https://media.gdcvault.com/gdc2017/GameNarrativeReview/Calvin_Walantus_UCSC_GDC_Narrative.pdf)). A sensible pattern is to state the item's function first and add one oblique lore fact, keeping mechanical text separate so balance patches don't force lore rewrites. Read-only dialogue boxes are bound by geometry. Pokémon's two-line frames required writers to break every line by hand ([Blake Crosley](https://blakecrosley.com/blog/pixel-art-interfaces-on-iphone)), and Fire Emblem's box widened to fit the longest line in a sequence, so writers balanced line lengths ([FEUniverse](https://feuniverse.us/t/dialogue-box-lengths-in-depth/900)). Xbox guidance requires a speaker label at every change of speaker and forbids color as the only cue ([XAG 104](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/104)). Writing each box as one thought ending at a natural pause lets the player's advance press fall on a beat. Efforts, vendor pools, achievements, loading tips and diegetic texts and emails have almost no citable craft literature. The conventions in the table for them are reasoned extrapolations from the bark and frequency rules.

## Production rules turn a good line into a shippable one

### The voice-over script is a sortable spreadsheet

Game VO is recorded out of order, quickly, from rows in a spreadsheet, so every row has to stand on its own. Chandler's column set remains the reference. *Character* names are spelled consistently so filters work, with a separate *Voice Actor* column because one actor often plays several roles. The *Cue* holds the spoken text only, with notes kept out of it. *Context* gives the overall situation and *Trigger* the specific prompt. *Inflection* uses plain, repeated emotion words so the column sorts. Then come *Location*, a sortable *Area* number, an audio *Effect* (radio futz, echo), a *Filename*, and a *Line Choice* column marking the selected take. Actors' printouts and implementers' printouts show different subsets ([Chandler, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/audio/screen-play-documenting-voice-assets)). Filenames encode structure, so `m01-a02-lan01` is mission 1, area 2, Lancelot, line 1, and alternates take letter suffixes. In one of Chandler's projects, two files sharing a cue name crashed the game, and lines missing from the master sheet produced false QA bugs and missed localization ([Chandler, Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/organizing-and-formatting-game-dialogue)). Line IDs should be frozen before audio is generated and never reused or renumbered, and teams exporting to engines should use TSV rather than CSV because dialogue contains commas ([Crawford](https://daracrawford.com/audio-blog/game-audio-project-management-part-3-dialogue-planning)).

Context matters because actors often arrive knowing little. They may be under NDA and not even know the game's title before the session ([Schmidt, GameSoundCon](https://www.gamesoundcon.com/post/2019/09/09/dialogue-for-video-games-11-things-you-should-know)). Nicholas Kraak's direction advice is to give a short overview of where the scene sits in the story, avoid result-oriented notes (asking for "funny") and line readings, and frame direction as *playable verbs* such as intimidate, comfort or tease ([How To Write a Game](https://howtowriteagame.substack.com/p/how-to-direct-your-games-voice-actors)). Chandler's preference for a small controlled vocabulary of emotion words looks like it conflicts with this, but the two coexist. A controlled Inflection column handles batching, and a free-text Direction column written as verbs handles performance. Studios typically record **two to three takes** per line ([Crawford](https://daracrawford.com/audio-blog/game-audio-project-management-part-3-dialogue-planning)). Repetitive systemic lines need variants, and the same line may need separate exertion and idle performances ([Schmidt](https://www.gamesoundcon.com/post/2019/09/09/dialogue-for-video-games-11-things-you-should-know)). Shouting, creature sounds, combat efforts and death cries strain the voice, union contracts cap vocally stressful work per session, and efforts are scheduled at the end of sessions ([Schmidt](https://www.gamesoundcon.com/post/2019/09/09/dialogue-for-video-games-11-things-you-should-know); [Crawford](https://daracrawford.com/audio-blog/game-audio-project-management-part-3-dialogue-planning)). Crawford estimates session length as **total words × takes ÷ words per minute**, and recommends text-to-speech placeholder VO to test timing and triggers before final recording ([Crawford](https://daracrawford.com/audio-blog/game-audio-project-management-part-3-dialogue-planning)). For an agent, this means emitting one row per line with a stable ID, speaker, addressee, clean spoken text, context naming the preceding line or event, a verb-based direction, a controlled emotion value, intensity, effect and notes, with no stage directions embedded in the spoken text.

### Subtitles and on-screen text come with hard numbers

Accessibility guidance gives the firmest numbers in the field. They matter because subtitle use is mainstream: Ian Hamilton reports that more than **60% of Assassin's Creed Origins players** turned subtitles on ([CMF](https://cmf-fmc.ca/now-next/articles/subtitles-in-video-games-the-best-practices/)).

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Lines per subtitle | 2, with 3 only in exceptional cases | [GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/if-any-subtitles-captions-are-used-present-them-in-a-clear-easy-to-read-way/); [XAG 104](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/104) |
| Characters per line | 38 (Hamilton, BBC-derived); ≤40 (GAG, XAG); 42 (Netflix) | [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/10-golden-rules-on-subtitles-for-games); [Netflix TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide) |
| Minimum on-screen time | ~1 s per line; ~2.5 s per full subtitle | [80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/10-golden-rules-on-subtitles-for-games) |
| Reading speed | 160–180 wpm (BBC); ≤20 characters/s adults, ≤17 children (Netflix) | [Clevercast copy of BBC](https://clevercast.com/bbc-subtitling-guidelines); [Netflix TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide) |
| Subtitle size | ≥46 px at 1080p (GAG); XAG floor 26 px on console, scalable to 200% | [GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/if-any-subtitles-captions-are-used-present-them-in-a-clear-easy-to-read-way/); [XAG 101](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/101) |
| Speaker label | At every speaker change and after a 1–2 minute pause; color never the only cue; context tags such as "(on radio)" | [XAG 104](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/104) |
| Text blocks | ≤80 characters per line (≤40 CJK); line spacing ≥1.5; sentence case | [XAG 101](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/101) |
| Defaults | Subtitles on, or offered before any audio plays | [XAG 104](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/104) |

Line breaks follow sense rather than the auto-wrap. Netflix breaks after punctuation and before conjunctions or prepositions. It never separates an article from its noun, a first name from a surname, or a pronoun from its verb, and it prefers bottom-heavy two-line shapes ([Netflix TTSG](https://partnerhelp.netflixstudios.com/hc/en-us/articles/217350977-English-USA-Timed-Text-Style-Guide)). Captions add important non-speech sound with spatial arrows, and XAG wants caption categories that can be toggled independently ([XAG 104](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/gaming/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines/104)). Two principles pull against each other here. Hamilton wants subtitles to match what was actually spoken, since actors deviate from the script ([80.lv](https://80.lv/articles/10-golden-rules-on-subtitles-for-games)), while the Game Accessibility Guidelines allow condensing only when speech outruns reading speed ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/ensure-that-subtitlescaptions-are-cut-down-to-and-presented-at-an-appropriate-words-per-minute-for-the-target-age-group)). An 84-character subtitle needs roughly 4–5 seconds at 17–20 characters per second, so the writer's real fix is tighter lines that split cleanly at clause boundaries. The same guidelines rate plain language as a basic requirement and note that **14% of US and UK adults** read below age eleven ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/use-simple-clear-language/)). No industry standard exists for characters per dialogue box. It depends on the font and the box, so an agent should take the box budget as an input rather than assume one.

### Localization punishes fragments, puns and English-only grammar

Text grows in translation, and short strings grow most. IBM data reproduced by the W3C gives the following average expansion from English by source length ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/article-text-size)).

| English source length | Typical translated length |
|---|---|
| ≤10 characters | 200–300% |
| 11–20 | 180–200% |
| 21–30 | 160–180% |
| 31–50 | 140–160% |
| 51–70 | 151–170% |
| >70 | 130% |

A study of general software text found English to French or Spanish running about **+20%** and English to German or Italian about **+17%** ([Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2016/06/20/localization_guidelines)). Lower-authority game guides cite up to +35% for German, so estimates vary with the source and the type of text. Abbreviating to fit a box backfires because other languages may lack the abbreviation ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/article-text-size)). The bigger danger is grammatical. Building sentences from code fragments breaks because word order, prepositions and conjugation change across languages, so strings must be whole sentences with named placeholders the translator can move ([Crowdin](https://crowdin.com/blog/game-localization)). One English word used in different roles, such as "Attack" as a button, a stat and a pop-up, needs separate string IDs ([Crowdin](https://crowdin.com/blog/game-localization)). Plurals vary far more than English suggests. Unicode CLDR assigns English and German two plural categories (one, other), French three, Russian, Polish and Czech four, Arabic six, and Japanese, Chinese and Korean one ([Unicode CLDR](https://www.unicode.org/cldr/charts/latest/supplemental/language_plural_rules.html)). Yarn Spinner's documentation states that `<<if $count == 1>>` logic works only for English-like plural rules, and that its `[plural]` and `[select]` markup let each locale choose its own forms ([Yarn Spinner](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/3.1/write-yarn-scripts/advanced-scripting/markup)). Translators also need context. A common bug is a female character referring to herself in the masculine because the translator couldn't see who was speaking to whom ([Allcorrect](https://allcorrectgames.com/?p=6072)). Humor needs annotation explaining its function. Sega's Scott Strichart says a literal translation kills Yakuza's comedy, so translators add a little spin and are paired with editors ([Newsweek](https://www.newsweek.com/entertainment/video-games/sega-yakuza-games-second-life-west-1091255); [Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/q-a-translating-the-humor-tone-of-i-yakuza-i-games-for-the-west)). Final Fantasy XIV's localization lead uses localization as a continuity check against older content ([Fandom](https://www.fandom.com/articles/koji-fox-stormblood-lore-localization)). Dubbing adds timing tiers: a soft limit of ±10% of the source audio length, an exact-length hard limit, sound-sync, and full lip-sync ([Logrus IT](https://games.logrusit.com/en/news/how-is-audio-localized-for-games)). English source lines that are no denser than they need to be make all of those tiers easier.

### Ink and Yarn Spinner encode structure as plain text

Writer-facing tools fall into two families. Ink (inkle) and Yarn Spinner are plain-text scripting languages that writers can edit directly. articy:draft and Chat Mapper are graph databases that export to engines and to spreadsheets for localization and VO ([articy](https://www.articy.com/help/adx/Localization_WhatsThis.html); [Chat Mapper](https://docs.chatmapper.com/game-developers/)). Fountain is a plain-text screenplay format suited to linear cinematics ([Fountain](https://fountain.io/syntax)). Ink organizes content into knots (`=== name ===`) and stitches (`= name`). Diverts (`->`) jump between them, choices are once-only (`*`) or sticky (`+`), gathers (`-`) rejoin branches, `~` lines run logic, `{a|b}`-style alternatives produce sequences, cycles and shuffles, and `#` tags carry metadata the game reads but the player never sees ([ink documentation](https://github.com/inkle/ink/blob/master/Documentation/WritingWithInk.md)). Ink treats a `Name:` prefix as plain text for the game to parse, and its official documentation describes no built-in line-ID system ([RunningYourInk](https://github.com/inkle/ink/blob/master/Documentation/RunningYourInk.md)). Community tools such as LocaliserLib add per-line ID tags ([NuGet](https://www.nuget.org/packages/wildwinter.LocaliserLib)). Yarn Spinner, whose current documentation is version 3.1 for the Yarn Spinner 3 line, uses `title:` nodes bounded by `---` and `===`. It marks speakers with `Name:` and options with `->` plus indentation. It has `<<if>>`, `<<jump>>` and `<<detour>>` commands, `<<once>>` blocks, `=>` line groups from which the engine picks one line (useful for barks), and `#line:` IDs that the tooling requires on every line before it will generate a localization strings file ([Yarn Spinner](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/3.1/write-yarn-scripts/scripting-fundamentals/lines-nodes-and-options); [Yarn Spinner, once](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/3.1/write-yarn-scripts/scripting-fundamentals/once); [Yarn Spinner, line groups](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/3.1/write-yarn-scripts/scripting-fundamentals/line-groups); [Yarn Spinner, tags](https://docs.yarnspinner.dev/3.1/write-yarn-scripts/advanced-scripting/tags-metadata)). Godot's Dialogue Manager uses the dialogue text itself as its translation key unless a line carries an explicit `[ID:...]` ([Dialogue Manager docs](https://www.mintlify.com/nathanhoad/godot_dialogue_manager/usage/translations)), so any later edit to an un-IDed line orphans its translations. The two sketches below show the same small guard scene in each language. They were assembled from documented constructs and have not been compiled, and the `#id:` tag in the Ink version is a convention, not a built-in feature.

```ink
VAR met_guard = false
-> gate

=== gate ===
Guard: {met_guard: Back again.|Halt. State your business.} #id:gate_001
~ met_guard = true
* [Ask about the curfew.] Player: When does the curfew lift? #id:gate_002
    Guard: When the Guild says it lifts. #id:gate_003
* [Offer coin.] -> bribe
+ [Leave.] -> END
- Guard: Move along. #id:gate_004
-> END

=== bribe ===
Guard: Put that away before someone sees. #id:gate_005
-> END
```

```yarn
title: Gate
---
<<declare $pc_pronoun = "nb">>
<<once>>
    Guard: Halt. State your business. #line:gate001
<<else>>
    Guard: Back again. #line:gate002
<<endonce>>
-> Player: When does the curfew lift? #line:gate003
    Guard: When the Guild says it lifts. #line:gate004
-> Player: (Offer coin.) #line:gate005
    <<jump Bribe>>
-> Player: Never mind. #line:gate006
Guard: [select value={$pc_pronoun} m="He's" f="She's" nb="They're" /] trouble, that one. #line:gate007
===
title: GuardIdle
---
=> Guard: Cold night for it. #line:idle001
=> Guard: Keep your hands where I can see them. #line:idle002
```

The lowest common denominator across these tools is `Speaker: text #tags`, with VO, emotion and localization metadata in tags and never inside the spoken text. Ink's inline conditionals sit inside strings, so translators receive logic along with text. Gendered variants are therefore safest as whole-line alternatives, which matches the localizers' advice.

## The worst dialogue failures were invisible in the script

The best-documented failures in recent games are not bad sentences. They are mismatches that appear only in context: in the build, at volume, or in another language. The table names each anti-pattern, the documented case, and a check that would have caught it. Several of the cases are disputed among critics, and the table notes where.

| Anti-pattern | Documented case | Check before shipping |
|---|---|---|
| Ludonarrative dissonance | BioShock asks for empathy while harvesting pays ([Inverse](https://www.inverse.com/gaming/ludonarrative-dissonance-meaning-video-games-origins-definition)); some scholars read it as deliberate | What did the player most likely just do, and does the line still hold? |
| Register mismatch | Forspoken's modern snark in a fantasy world ([Den of Geek](https://www.denofgeek.com/games/forspokens-dialog-debate-controversy-examples)); backlash disputed | Justify every contemporary idiom against setting and speaker |
| Tonal whiplash | Saints Row (2022): quips praised, tone uneven ([TechRadar](https://www.techradar.com/reviews/saints-row)) | Mark each scene's emotional peak; the next line must not deflate it by default |
| Joke without a target | Borderlands 3's streamer parody ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/borderlands-3-review/)); defended by [Dread Central](https://www.dreadcentral.com/reviews/473547/borderlands-3-review-another-one-but-actually-its-quite-good/) | State in one sentence what the joke says about its target |
| Hint nag | God of War Ragnarök ([TheGamer](https://www.thegamer.com/god-of-war-ragnarok-devs-unaware-chatty-companions-until-launch/)); Horizon's stash lines | Gate on stuck evidence, escalate, cap, expose a setting |
| Bark wear-out | Skyrim's arrow in the knee ([Bethesda](https://elderscrolls.bethesda.net/en-EU/news/how-an-arrow-in-the-knee-made-its-way-to-skyrim)) | Estimate plays per hour; once-only flags and cooldowns for distinctive lines |
| Paraphrase mismatch | Fallout 4's wheel ([TechRaptor](https://techraptor.net/content/fallout-4s-dialogue-wheel-short-answers-make-no-sense)) | Put each label beside its full line; predict intent, tone, consequence |
| Overlap and mix collisions | Suicide Squad: Kill the Justice League, with several conversations at once ([Kotaku](https://kotaku.com/suicide-squad-justice-league-review-is-it-good-batman-1851222047)) | List lines that can fire together; set priorities; keep required info out of interruptible lines |
| Silent trigger failure | BG3's ~1,500 untriggered companion lines ([Bell of Lost Souls](https://www.belloflostsouls.net/2023/08/baldurs-gate-3-more-than-1500-lines-of-dialogue-to-be-restored-in-patch-2.html)) | Keep trigger conditions per line so coverage can be tested |
| Wall of text | Planescape: Torment's own late worry about volume ([GameBanshee](https://www.gamebanshee.com/k3hdd)) | Word counts per node; split or cut |
| "As you know, Bob" | Classic exposition trope | Does the listener already know it? Reroute through someone who doesn't |
| Translation-hostile strings | Masculine self-reference by female speakers ([Allcorrect](https://allcorrectgames.com/?p=6072)) | Whole sentences, plural/select markup, speaker and listener metadata, pun notes |

The acclaimed cases share four habits. First, they make the dialogue system itself carry character, as with Disco Elysium's skills, Pentiment's fonts and Oxenfree's interruptions. Second, they budget reactivity so lines rarely repeat or ignore state. Firewatch's designer Sean Vanaman described a script of about **1,000 printed pages** of which players hear **under half**, with the game choosing the most fitting next line from earlier choices ([Twinfinite](https://twinfinite.net/news/firewatch-has-a-1000-page-script-says-designer/)). Third, they let a constraint carry emotion, as with Firewatch's radio-only link, God of War's boat, and Pentiment's lack of voice acting. Fourth, they iterate in context. The Witcher 3's Bloody Baron questline went through several iterations before designers approved it ([Shacknews](https://www.shacknews.com/article/94509/witcher-3-quest-designers-talk-design-process-most-difficult-quest-to-create)). God of War (2018) cut an early scene of Atreus screaming at Kratos after repeated feedback that it broke the flow ([TIME](https://partners.time.com/5248154/god-of-war-kratos-atreus)). Portal 2's narrative premise was rebuilt after playtesters reacted badly to an early concept without its core cast ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2012-03-08-the-importance-of-playtesting-what-portal-2-could-have-been.html)). Pentiment's font changes confused testers at first and became legible through iteration, though calligraphic text remains an accessibility cost ([GameBanshee](https://gamebanshee.com/96xr8); [Well-Played](https://www.well-played.com.au/pentiment-preview-a-murder-mystery-scratched-in-ink/)). Even acclaimed work earns self-criticism. Larian's Adam Smith later said BG3's companions lack diversity of motivation because they share one premise ([FRVR](https://frvr.com/blog/baldurs-gate-3-writing-director-says-the-game-lacks-companion-diversity-and-overlap-compared-to-other-crpgs/)), and Hades' Kasavin said the team would probably have balked had they known the cost at the start ([Screenhub](https://www.screenhub.com.au/news-article/features/digital/jini-maxwell/hades-greg-kasavin-breaks-down-supergiants-unique-approach-to-narrative-262459)).

Professional review therefore works on four layers. The first is *hearing it*. A GDC 2026 session proposes table reads with writers, actors and developers as a beta test of the script ([GDC Vault](https://gdcvault.com/play/1035676/Page-to-Booth-Table-Reading)), and the Twelve Minutes team ran one with an orientation followed by excerpts arranged into a linear sequence ([Twelve Minutes](https://twelveminutesgame.com/?p=2161)). The second is *playing it*, in long sessions with players who are genuinely stuck, which is exactly the condition Ragnarök's playtests missed. The third is *tracing it*: narrative QA of branch and state permutations, as with BG3's dedicated testing of ending permutations ([Yahoo/PC Gamer](https://malaysia.news.yahoo.com/larian-gave-baldurs-gate-3-022539159.html)). The fourth is *translating it*, using localization as a second editorial pass. The critics' implicit criteria condense into a six-part rubric, which is a synthesis rather than a published standard. *Fit* asks whether the line suits the setting and tone. *Function* asks whether every line does a job. *Fidelity* asks whether label matches line and line matches state. *Frequency* asks whether the line survives repetition. *Flow* covers pacing, interruption and agency. *Fluency* asks whether it reads aloud well and translates. The reviews show critics punishing register errors far more loudly than prose errors. Forspoken's lines were grammatical; they were in the wrong voice for the world.

## Language models fail exactly where the craft is hardest

The empirical record on AI-written fiction is consistent across model families, and it lines up closely with what game writers prize. In *Art or Artifice?* (CHI 2024), ten creative writers rated stories on 14 tests of creative writing. New Yorker stories passed **84.7%** of tests, while Claude 1.3 passed **30.0%**, GPT-4 **27.9%** and GPT-3.5 **8.7%**. Experts described the AI dialogue as lacking subtext and relying on direct, expository speech. They found characters over-explaining their situations and feelings, endings that were moralistic or unrealistically happy, clichéd and repeated phrasing, and characters flattened into stereotype. No model's judgments of the tests correlated positively with the experts' ([arXiv 2309.14556](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.14556v2)). The follow-up LAMP study had professionals edit **1,057** LLM-written paragraphs from GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet and Llama 3.1 70B. Writers agreed on seven categories of flaw: cliché, unnecessary exposition, purple prose, poor sentence structure, lack of specificity, awkward word choice and tense inconsistency. The most frequent edits were awkward word choice (**28%**), sentence structure (**20%**), unnecessary exposition (**18%**) and cliché (**17%**). No model was clearly better than the others, and the study found that models reveal subtext instead of implying it ([arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5)). *Too Good to be Bad* (Findings of ACL 2026) found that role-play fidelity declines as a character's morality declines. Models do worst on deceitful and manipulative traits and replace nuanced malevolence with surface aggression, with highly safety-aligned models faring especially badly ([arXiv 2511.04962](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.04962)). Homogenization runs deep. GPT-4 and Llama 3 reuse the same idiosyncratic plot elements across generations and across models ([arXiv 2501.00273](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.00273v1)). A Cornell analysis of **20,000** story outputs from ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and OLMo found that nearly **90%** contained at least one of eleven recurring tokens, among them the names Elias, Mara and Elara, and more than half featured a lighthouse ([Cornell Chronicle](https://news.cornell.edu/node/333804)). The *Antislop* work found some stock patterns appearing more than **1,000 times** more often in LLM output than in human text ([arXiv 2510.15061](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.15061)).

Mapped onto game dialogue, these findings mark specific danger zones. Every character drifts toward the model's default register, which defeats the cover-the-names test. Emotions get named, which is the opposite of the subtext craft. Scenes resolve too quickly into apology and validation, so the tension the scene was built on disappears. Villains, liars, cheating merchants and companions who should disagree go soft. Fantasy names and settings fall back on the same defaults. One caveat matters: none of these studies tested barks or branching conversations directly. The evidence comes from short fiction and role-play, and transferring it to game dialogue is inference, though a well-supported one.

### What studios, unions and storefronts have decided

Industry practice has settled into a pattern: human writers own story and character, and AI drafts variants under human selection. Ubisoft's Ghostwriter, announced in 2023, generates first drafts of barks. The writer defines a character and an utterance type, picks between pairs of proposed variants, and edits freely ([Ubisoft](https://news.ubisoft.com/en-us/article/7Cm07zbBGy4Xml6WgYi25d/the-convergence-of-ai-and-creativity-introducing-ghostwriter)). Ubisoft said the tool would not be used for plot-central dialogue. Writer Alanah Pearce countered that editing AI lines takes longer than writing temp lines oneself ([VGC](https://www.videogameschronicle.com/news/ubisoft-developer-explains-its-ai-writing-tool-in-more-detail/)), and other critics warned it could close off the bark-writing jobs where junior writers traditionally start ([LevelUp](https://www.levelup.com/noticias/sus-juegos-seran-mas-genericos-fans-y-devs-arremeten-contra-ubisoft-ghostwriter/)). Ubisoft's experimental *Teammates* has writers author personalities and "fences" rather than every line ([Heise](https://heise.de/-11088815)). Runtime generation has produced public incidents. Players manipulated Fortnite's AI Darth Vader into profanity and slurs, Epic shipped hotfixes, and SAG-AFTRA filed an unfair labor practice charge ([PinkNews](https://www.thepinknews.com/2025/05/21/fortnite-darth-vader-ai-slurs/); [Insider Gaming](https://insider-gaming.com/fortnite-faces-unfair-labor-practice-charge-for-use-of-ai-darth-vader-voice/)). Embark defended Arc Raiders' text-to-speech voices as prototyping, then began re-recording lines with actors by March 2026 ([Push Square](https://www.pushsquare.com/news/2026/03/arc-raiders-replacing-some-of-its-controversial-ai-voices-with-better-human-ones)). Developer sentiment has turned sharply. In the GDC 2026 State of the Industry survey, **52%** of developers said generative AI is harming the industry, up from 18% in 2024, and **63%** of game design and narrative respondents said so. Among those who do use it, the top use was research and brainstorming at **81%** ([GDC](https://gdconf.com/article/gdc-2026-state-of-the-game-industry-reveals-impact-of-layoffs-generative-ai-and-more/)). SAG-AFTRA's 2025 interactive agreement, ratified by **95.04%** of members, requires informed, specific consent for digital voice replicas and disclosure of intended uses ([TheWrap](https://www.thewrap.com/sag-aftra-video-game-deal-details/)). Steam's January 2026 disclosure revision requires developers to disclose AI-generated content that players consume. It distinguishes *pre-generated* content shipped in game files from *live-generated* content, which requires guardrails and can be reported by players, and it exempts internal productivity tools ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Steam-updates-AI-disclosure-form-requiring-developers-to-report-visible-and-in-game-AI-but-not-background-tools.1206103.0.html)). Whether heavily human-rewritten AI drafts count as disclosable is unsettled. A skill should flag the question rather than answer it.

### Countermeasures that have evidence behind them

The evidence favors changing the process over rewording prompts. Generating several candidates and selecting with explicit criteria works. A reward model trained on expert edits reached **74%** accuracy at judging writing quality, and best-of-N revision selected by that model was preferred by experienced writers **66%** of the time (**72.2%** when the reward gap was large). The authors stress that this is still far from expert quality, and that baseline LLMs barely beat chance as judges ([arXiv 2504.07532](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.07532v3)). Generic self-editing prompts work poorly, taxonomy-guided LLM edits beat raw output, and few-shot LLM detectors of writing flaws reached only **0.46** precision ([arXiv 2409.14509](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.14509v5)). Asking the model for several responses with their probabilities, a method called *verbalized sampling*, increased output diversity **1.6–2.1×** on stories and jokes without loss of factuality or safety ([arXiv 2510.01171](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.01171v1)). Banning slop tokens directly becomes unusable at around 2,000 patterns, while a backtracking approach handled more than 8,000 ([ICLR 2026, Antislop](https://mlanthology.org/iclr/2026/paech2026iclr-antislop/)). At the agent level that translates into scanning drafts after generation and rewriting the flagged lines. RoleLLM elicited character speaking styles more faithfully when it retrieved the character's own material ([arXiv 2310.00746](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2310.00746)), which supports feeding voice profiles and sample lines into context. The villain findings imply that prompts must specify the *method* of harm, such as the lie, the leverage or the false concession, rather than asking for menace.

The game-writing craft already has tests that are explicit and checkable, which happens to be exactly what weak LLM judges need. The cover-the-names test catches homogenized voice. A forbidden-pattern scan catches "I feel X because Y" lines and named emotions. Mamet's three questions check whether a scene has a want. Accept, Reject and Deflect check whether the options differ. The label-beside-line comparison catches paraphrase drift. Frequency estimates catch wear-out. A cut-the-first-and-last-line pass catches throat-clearing and tidy moral endings. A blocklist of default names and settings (Elara, Elias, Mara, Lyra, lighthouses, keepers) together with the setting's own naming rules catches the homogenized-world problem.

## A dialogue skill should mirror the professional pipeline

The findings above fit together into an ordered workflow. Each stage exists because a documented failure happens when it is skipped. The table is a synthesis for condensing into an agent skill. The evidence column points back to the sections above.

| Stage | What the agent does | Grounding |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Intake | Ask for or read: the game's medium (voiced, text, subtitled), engine format, line type, trigger and frequency, box or subtitle budget, rating, localization languages, PC model and address rules, lore bible and glossary | Riot gates VO on backstory, personality and goals; line type depends on trigger |
| 2. Voice profiles | Build or load a profile per speaker (core, want/need, register, attitude, topics, quirks, never-says, stress behavior, roster contrast, sample lines), plus ordered-pair relationship notes | Riot, Hedlund, Nicklin, Egri, Weiland |
| 3. Structure and state | Choose the pattern; write a scene brief (purpose, beats, wants, state read and written); plan entry and exit variants, once-only topics, fallbacks, Accept/Reject/Deflect options | Ashwell, Short, Choice of Games, Ingold |
| 4. Draft | Open on the want; pursue it by tactics; track status; route exposition through need or ignorance; end on a hook; generate several candidates per line, deliberately varied | McKee, Mamet, Johnstone, Slabinski; verbalized sampling |
| 5. Type rules | Apply the line-type conventions: one fact per bark, distinctiveness inverse to frequency, resumable banter beats, escalating hints, objective text separate from flavor | Dansky, Anderson, Mikkelson, Ragnarök and Horizon cases |
| 6. Production pass | Enforce length budgets and clean break points; assign stable IDs; give each line speaker, addressee and genders, context, verb direction and emotion; whole sentences with placeholders; plural and select markup; annotate puns | Chandler, XAG and GAG, Netflix, W3C, CLDR, Yarn docs, Souto |
| 7. Checked critique | Run concrete tests (names hidden, named-emotion scan, slop and default-name scan, label versus line, frequency estimate, state preconditions, register fit, cut first and last lines) and select the best candidate rather than polishing the first | LAMP taxonomy, best-of-N, anti-pattern table |
| 8. Handoff | Deliver as drafts with open questions: proposed canon, consultation flags for identity or culture content, lines needing playtest or table read, AI-disclosure note | Blizzard historians, Tell Me Why, Ghostwriter practice, Steam 2026 rules |

Two defaults deserve emphasis because the evidence for them is strongest. The agent should treat a line's metadata as part of the line. A bark without a trigger and a frequency estimate, or a choice label without its spoken expansion, cannot be judged at all. The agent should also never certify its own output as finished, authentic or canonical. Expert studies show models cannot judge creative quality reliably, and the most damaging industry failures surfaced only in play.

## Conclusion

The central change in perspective is that the unit of game dialogue is not the sentence but the line in its runtime context: trigger, frequency, state, speaker, addressee, medium and language. A sentence that would pass any screenwriting class can still fail in a game by firing too often, contradicting a choice the player just made, mislabeling what the hero will say, or breaking grammar in Polish. This explains why the field's most useful advice comes as structural rules (three options as three different moves, distinctiveness inverse to frequency, fixed subtext with variable text, a fallback before any specific line) rather than stylistic ones. It also explains why the decisive review happens in the build rather than on the page.

For an AI agent, a second finding matters as much. The craft's own tools happen to be the explicit, checkable criteria that compensate for language models' documented weaknesses. Voice matrices counter homogenized voice. Status and tactic counter therapeutic softness. Want-driven exposition counters on-the-nose explanation. Selection against concrete tests counters unreliable self-judgment. What remains genuinely uncertain is quantitative. No public standard exists for variants per trigger, words per node, hint timing or bark cooldowns, and no study has yet measured LLM performance on game dialogue itself. A skill built on this report should therefore expose those numbers as tunable parameters, prefer generating many candidates and selecting to polishing one, and end every delivery by naming what only a human playtest, table read or cultural consultant can verify.
