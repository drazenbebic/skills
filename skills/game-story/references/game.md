# Story in play

What makes a game's story different from a film's or a novel's: it lives in verbs, spaces and loops,
it is mostly middle, and most players never see its end. Sources are named briefly so a rule can be
traced.

## Games are mostly Act 2

- A typical game is almost entirely Act 2, with Acts 1 and 3 reduced to cutscene decoration; the
  "refusal of the call" doesn't survive, because a hero who refuses is a player asked whether to keep
  playing (Skolnick, GDC 2007).
- **Serial structure fits better than three acts**: a season arc carried by relationships, plus
  episodes that stand alone (Abernathy & Rouse, GDC 2014). Gulino's sequences — each a mini-story with
  its own question and partial resolution — map onto chapters, regions or run tiers.
- **The accordion**: plot points stay fixed and the middles expand (Jenkins). So the macro-structure is
  a short list of fixed, load-bearing turns, with chapter-sized stretches between them, each carrying
  its own question, escalation and payoff.

## The two contracts

Hocking's BioShock critique (2007) is the canonical case: the ludic contract said *seek power and you
will progress* — harvesting was rewarded — while the narrative contract said *help Atlas and you will
progress*, with no way to refuse him. When the twist came, "the game openly mocks us".

- **State both contracts in one sentence each**: what the systems reward; what the story asks the
  protagonist to value. Resolve any opposition, or name the beat that confronts it. Dissonance can be
  the subject — Spec Ops: The Line (Williams, GDC 2013) — but only when the story acknowledges it.
- **The theme rides a verb.** The documented successes found the theme in the mechanics, often because
  the mechanics came first: Papers, Please gave every new inspection rule a story reason (a body
  scanner after an attack); Undertale's mercy grew from enjoying *talking* to monsters; Outer Wilds
  made knowledge the only progression; Hades turned dying into the story's engine.
- **Fix the system, not the script**, when a mechanic tells the wrong story. Journey's players could
  shove each other around in a game about companionship; the team removed collision and made
  companions recharge each other.
- **Check**: every major theme maps to at least one verb, rule or world-state response — and list any
  cheap affordance that lets the player act against it.

## Delivery modes

Jenkins's four kinds of spatial story: **evoked** (a space calls up a story players know), **enacted**
(players perform events), **embedded** (story scattered for players to reconstruct) and **emergent**
(story arising from systems). Each makes its own demand on the bible:

- **Redundancy.** Any essential plot point is conveyed in at least three ways, because players miss
  clues — a "law of three" from soap-opera practice that Jenkins cites. The bible's floor is two
  carriers per must-know fact; give the central reveals three.
- **Environment.** Set a governing story the whole team follows, never break the world's rules, and
  answer "Where am I?" within about 15 seconds (Carson, 2000). Each staged scene needs a readable chain
  of events, and must never imply something the player's systems couldn't do (Smith & Worch, GDC 2010).
  A landscape where every rock tells a story leaves no room for the player's own (Jenkins).
- **Embedded and fragmented lore** needs the complete hidden truth written down plus a ledger of which
  fragment reveals what, and where. A long-time FromSoftware translator suspects much Souls lore exists
  only in Miyazaki's head; Team Cherry found secrets meant to last weeks solved within an hour.
  Dataminers read everything, so optional lore never contradicts canon.
- **Knowledge gates** need a dependency graph with more than one route to each gate. Outer Wilds'
  early opening crashed an object into the village and fell flat — players didn't know the village yet.
- **Run-based story** needs a fiction for death and repetition, eligibility rules, per-visit caps and an
  ending gated on story state rather than on a single win (Hades; Short's "loop and grow";
  `references/genres.md` → *Roguelites*).

## Channels

Every channel trades authorial control against attention; keep anything critical off optional
channels. Found text should be interesting, "but not as interesting as being sucked into a cyclone"
(Beachum, Outer Wilds). A synthesis, not a published standard:

| Channel | Best for | Avoid for |
|---|---|---|
| Cutscene, authored camera | Irreversible turns, deaths, reveals that need framing | Exposition the player could learn by playing; anything before first control |
| In-engine scene, portraits and text | Relationships, walk-and-talk, tension | Long information dumps |
| Environment and props | Backstory, world history, "what happened here" | Facts needed to proceed, unless carried elsewhere too |
| Item, codex, found text | Optional depth and mystery | Core plot; the protagonist's main motive |
| Narration, radio | Commentary that reacts to play | Moments that need visual drama |
| Systems (world state, music, who is present) | Consequences of player conduct; theme | Precise information |
| Briefings and debriefs | What a mission means; the guild's record | Repeating what the scene just showed |
| Recaps | Returning players; accessibility | Replacing delivery in play |

Briefings and debriefs, and portrait-and-text scenes, have no craft literature of their own; treat
them as scenes with the same tests.

## The protagonist type

Whether the player character speaks is a structural decision, not a voice decision.

- **Silent**: Nintendo tried giving Link lines in Echoes of Wisdom and found it "felt really wrong".
  A silent hero's story is told through how others treat them (Laidlaw on Gordon Freeman and Alyx).
  No beat may require them to voice a feeling.
- **Defined**: allows an authored arc but opens a gap between what the character wants and what the
  player wants. Align the player with them early (Druckmann on The Last of Us's opening) and record,
  per act, the character's want, the player's objective, and whether they agree.
- **Player-made**: a hole at the centre unless an origin fills it. Divinity: Original Sin 2's custom
  characters had no story of their own; Baldur's Gate 3's Dark Urge is a customisable origin with a
  past to uncover. The spine must not depend on a backstory the player supplies.
- **Amnesia** has no developer defence on record; critics call it an exposition crutch the player sees
  through. Use a newcomer protagonist or a knowledgeable companion, and keep a per-beat ledger of what
  the protagonist knows against what the player knows.

## Choices are promises

The story-level failures are unpaid promises, not too few branches.

- **Mass Effect 3**: three games of choices felt ignored at the end; the free Extended Cut added
  epilogues showing how choices played out, and a fourth option — refusal.
- **Hades II**: its original ending gave the climactic choice to another character; Patch 1, about a
  month after the September 2025 release, moved it to the protagonist.
- **Kentucky Route Zero** avoids the problem by promising no consequences: its choices express
  character rather than change outcomes.
- **Checks**: every choice the game signals as consequential — in UI, origin choice or marketing — is
  in the promise ledger with a visible payoff; the endings reflect the biggest ones; the climax is
  performed or chosen by the player character, unless withholding it is the point and the player was
  aligned with the protagonist first.

## Pacing for players who stop

Completion data — reaching an ending, not understanding a story — shows most players stop early:
Assassin's Creed Valhalla's ~61-hour campaign finished by 15–17% against ~50% for shorter games such
as Spider-Man (MIDiA); a mean of 14% across 412 Steam games with a completion achievement (Bailey &
Miyata, 2017, preliminary); about 30% not finishing even the year's 13 most popular Xbox games (2008).
Length correlates only weakly with completion (r = −0.24 in one columnist's sample), so shortening
alone doesn't fix it.

- **Meaning before the midpoint**: the central question, the main relationship and the theme are
  legible by the middle of the beat map; at least one emotional payoff lands in the first session.
  The ending is for the minority who arrive.
- **Story as reward, in short segments.** Tell it "as the game happens" (Wolpaw, Valve). F.I.S.T.
  split its plot into about 50 segments over roughly 20 hours after players put the controller down
  to eat chips during a long scene — the only concrete figure; no standard exists for cutscene length
  or beat spacing, so treat numbers as parameters.
- **The beat chart** (Rogers; Filatov's version has one row per location: story events, new mechanic,
  progress, time, characters, audio) is scanned for overload, gaps and repeats. Don't stack a major
  tutorial on a major exposition beat; place non-interactive beats after accomplishments or over
  low-demand traversal.
- **Peaks need playtesting.** Journey's testers wanted it to end after the mountain; the final high
  didn't rise far enough above the low before it. Fixing it took an extra year. Mark the intended low
  and high; no mid-game peak outshines the finale.
- **A recap floor**: objectives reviewable at any time, and a way to revisit the story — cutscene
  replay or a written story-so-far (Xbox Accessibility Guideline 109).

## When narrative joins late

Writers are often hired after the game is designed (Pratchett); Uncharted 3 began from about a dozen
planned set pieces the story had to absorb. Amy Hennig's counter-rule: writers must know where act
breaks, obstacles, reversals and set pieces fall.

- Inventory the story space before proposing anything: cutscene slots, voice budget, environments,
  channels, what is built and locked.
- Tie beats to **functions and dependencies** ("needs the player to know F-x from B-y"), not only to
  locations, so missions can be reordered without breaking cause.
- The writer owns the characters; the narrative designer owns the player's experience of the story
  (Maloney & Stirpe). On small teams one person does both; the bible serves both.

## Checks

1. **Contracts**: both stated; compatible, or a beat confronts the gap.
2. **Verbs**: each major theme maps to a verb, rule or world-state response.
3. **Redundancy**: two carriers per must-know fact, three for central reveals; nothing critical only in
   optional or skippable text.
4. **Environment**: every staged scene implies only what the systems allow.
5. **Lore**: hidden truth written; fragment ledger; optional lore consistent with canon.
6. **Gates**: more than one route to each knowledge gate.
7. **Protagonist**: the type decided, and its constraint obeyed (silent never voices a feeling;
   defined has want vs. objective per act; player-made doesn't carry the spine).
8. **Promises**: every signalled choice paid visibly; the climax is the player character's.
9. **Midpoint**: question, relationship and theme legible by the middle; a payoff in the first session.
10. **Peaks**: the largest high in the final stretch; no tutorial stacked on exposition.
