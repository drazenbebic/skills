# Genres: how much story, and how it rides the loop

Genre sets the story load; within every genre, acclaimed games span the whole range, and what they
share is story tied to the loop. Sources are named briefly; much of the genre guidance rests on
reviews and a handful of games, and says so.

## Story load

No public dataset ranks genres by how much story players want. The best evidence is Quantic
Foundry's motivation model: **Story** (elaborate plots, interesting characters) is one of 12
motivations, sitting with **Fantasy** (which holds deep lore) in an immersion cluster. Players low on
Story prefer open-ended games with no overarching narrative and skip dialogue and quest text where they
can. In shooters, Competition against Story is the main axis dividing audiences.

- **Critical information never lives only in skippable text** — a large share of players will skip it.
- **The first hour is probationary.** A study of first-hour reviews (Cheung, Zimmermann & Nagappan,
  CHI PLAY 2014; mostly Xbox 360 shooters and action games) found a good narrative keeps players going
  through frustration, anticipated elements carry them forward, and "intrigue trumps enjoyment": cues
  of depth still to come beat momentary fun. One reviewer quit over cutscenes he thought unskippable —
  they could be skipped; he didn't know. Skipping must be possible **and obvious**.
- **Amount doesn't predict acclaim.** Dead Cells' lead designer wanted little or no story because lore
  slowed the loop between death and the next run; Hades runs to about 305,000 words. Supergiant treats a
  difficulty wall that strands story-minded players as a failure — hence Hades' God Mode.

The scale for STORY.md follows from the load (`references/bible.md` → *Scale*). A synthesis, not a
measured ranking:

| Genre | What the bible carries | Failure to check for |
|---|---|---|
| Puzzle, casual | Premise, tone, a light frame | Story interrupting the puzzle loop |
| Platformer, metroidvania | Theme in mechanics and hazards; lore in the environment | Expository dumps; story fighting play |
| Action, shooter | Clear motive and stakes, kept skippable | Unskippable, or not obviously skippable, scenes |
| Roguelite | A reason to return after each death; short hub beats; a large line pool | Repeated lines after a few deaths; story behind a skill wall |
| ARPG with procedural floors | Premise; lore escalating with depth; hub characters who react to progress | Every floor narratively the same |
| Cozy, life sim | Relationship arcs unlocked over time; an open ending for the protagonist only | Shallow villagers; dropped threads players invested in |
| JRPG, creature collector | A full bible: cast, arcs, an antagonist with a thesis, the creatures' mystery | The ladder–rival–evil-team template saying nothing |
| Horror | Place, wrongness, a late summoning | Dread built on jump scares |

**Puzzle games still need a thread.** Portal's playtests showed players needed narrative to hold them
past 15 to 30 minutes; the answer was short announcements per test chamber, not front-loaded
exposition.

## Roguelites

Two documented approaches:

- **Death advances the story.** Kasavin's early goal for Hades was "to take the pain out of dying and
  having to restart": story progress persists across deaths the way gear does elsewhere. Because death
  isn't final, the protagonist and bosses remember earlier runs; because the game can be played
  indefinitely, players shouldn't hit repeated dialogue soon. Events are served when their run-state
  conditions hold, weighted by designers; only a couple of moments have fixed order. The ending is
  layered: credits on the tenth escape, a true epilogue behind further relationship requirements.
- **Story stays in fragments**, out of the loop (Dead Cells, Slay the Spire).

Between them, Darkest Dungeon uses one narrator who reacts to outcomes, plus systems (stress,
affliction) that generate story. Returnal's time loop let its team hide layers players uncover
through repetition — a tightrope between clarity and mystery, in its narrative designer's words.

- **Probabilistic gating needs a visible sign.** One Hades II player unlocked three major side stories
  only after about 105 runs and had assumed they were bugged.
- **For a procedural descent**, the sources suggest: hub characters who react to the deepest floor
  reached, the last cause of death and the number of returns, with events gated on state and weighted;
  lore fragments keyed to depth bands so revelations escalate; authored beats on fixed milestone floors
  between generated ones (inferred from Hades' fixed bosses, not stated by a developer); an ending in
  layers.

## Creature collectors

Critics pan the default template — badge ladder, rival, evil team, legendary creature — when it says
nothing: Temtem's story was called "cookie cutter", Coromon's flat; Digimon World 2 sits at a Metacritic
42 (from five reviews), for pacing and repetition. The praised entries give the story an argument
about creatures and people, or build the bond into a mechanic:

- **Pokémon Black and White**'s liberation philosophy is often called the series' best story —
  though Kotaku notes it stops short of its radical conclusion and folds the idealist into another
  villain's scheme.
- **Cassette Beasts**: the relationship with a human partner unlocks and scales fusion power — the bond
  is literally a combat stat.
- **Monster Hunter Stories 2** is about the responsibility of a power that is neither good nor bad.
- **Shin Megami Tensei**'s demon negotiation came from frustration that tabletop goblins were only loot,
  not beings to talk to.
- **Digimon World 1**'s memory-less creatures regain themselves when recruited and rebuild the city:
  recruiting *is* the story.

No developer talk on creature-collector narrative design was found; that evolution or fusion carries
theme is critics' and fans' interpretation. Checks:

1. Does the antagonist hold an argument about what people owe the creatures they use — and does the
   game take it seriously to the end?
2. Is the creatures' own nature a mystery revealed in stages?
3. Does at least one roster mechanic (catching, bonding, evolving, fusing) carry the theme, with a story
   consequence?
4. Do the ladder, the rival and the opposing faction each do thematic work, or are they furniture?
5. Does the hub visibly change as the story advances?

## Horror and folk horror

- **The folk horror chain** (Scovell, as described in academic sources): a landscape that shapes the
  identity of those who live there → isolation → skewed belief and morality → a happening or summoning.
  Thurgill's critique: it goes wrong when rural is treated as the same thing as landscape. One critic's
  games-ready test: the land is hungry, and complicit insiders ask *who* will die, not whether the rite
  is wrong.
- **Regional, not imported.** Mundaun built its horror from a real valley its developer has visited
  since childhood, hand-pencilled textures and voice acting entirely in Romansh; some characters are
  Swiss folklore regulars, others invented. Brian Gomez recalls telling the Silent Hill: Downpour team in
  Brno to stop being "Japanese scary" and be "Czech scary".
- **A named tale, deliberately changed.** Morteshka took One-Eyed Likho from Afanasyev's 19th-century
  collection and recorded its changes — a tailor quickly disposed of in the tale becomes a surviving
  companion.
- **Dread over jump scares**: Pathologic 2 aims for constant low-level dread; an academic reading of
  Blasphemous treats its death-and-resurrection cycle as penance built into the mechanics.
- **Against misery fatigue** across a long dark game: Darkest Dungeon tunes crit chances, art, light
  and narration to one message — the player is vulnerable — and rations triumphs, reversals and
  gallows wit (`references/particulars.md` → *Tone*).
- Balkan-folklore games so far are small indies without critical reception, and no source names which
  South Slavic collections suit a game: that choice needs research and a tradition-bearer.

Checks for a folk-horror bible: the premise passes the chain; what the land is hungry for, and who
pays; every folklore element traced to a named source with its deliberate changes; horror regional in
language, ritual and landscape; the villagers' beliefs logical from inside; dread from place, guilt and
system.
