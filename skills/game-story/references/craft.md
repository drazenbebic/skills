# Craft: building a story that holds

The best-sourced construction rules, as checks a beat map can be run against. Sources are named
briefly so a rule can be traced; the project's own rules beat every default here.

## Structure models are diagnostics

Three acts, Gulino's eight sequences, Truby's steps, Harmon's circle, Save the Cat, the Hero's
Journey, kishōtenketsu — each describes patterns in successful stories; none has been validated as a
recipe. Underneath, they test for the same functions: a status quo with a lack, a disruption, a goal
and plan, escalating opposition, a crisis or lowest point, a climactic choice, a changed equilibrium.

- **Use them to find a missing function, never as a template.** Suderman found Save the Cat's beats,
  in order and near their page targets, across a season of blockbusters, and argued that scenes
  written to hit a beat feel arbitrary.
- "The hero's journey" in game-industry talk usually means Vogler's 1985 studio memo, not Campbell.
- **Kishōtenketsu** is not "plot without conflict"; it isn't driven by one central confrontation, and
  its turn recontextualises rather than clashes.
- **Gathering stories** have a legitimate shape of their own (Le Guin's "carrier bag"). For an
  exploration or collection game, ask what the gathering adds up to, not where the final fight is.

## Cause, not sequence

Between two beats the word is "but" or "therefore", never "and then" (Parker & Stone at NYU, 2011,
known through press reports of the class). Forster's "the queen died of grief" and Aristotle's
ranking of episodic plots as the worst say the same.

**The deletion test**: delete a beat. If no later beat changes, it isn't part of the chain — cut it,
or connect it.

> *Episodic*: the courier reaches the port, **and then** meets a smuggler, **and then** raiders
> attack, **and then** she finds the relic.
> *Causal*: the courier reaches the port, **but** the harbour is sealed, **therefore** she bargains
> with a smuggler, **but** his price is the relic map, **therefore** she steals it from the archive,
> **but** the theft trips the wards that let the raiders in.

The attack is now the consequence of her choice, not a coincidence.

## Every unit turns a value

A scene is an action through conflict that turns a value-charged condition — alive/dead,
trusted/betrayed, free/captive — and a scene whose value ends where it began is a non-event (McKee).
In a game the unit is the beat: name the value, its charge on entry and on exit. A beat whose
**Change** field can't be written is a candidate for cutting.

## Escalation and stakes

- **Try–fail cycles**: each solution creates the next problem (Kowal, Writing Excuses).
- **Same stake, worse failure**: escalate the cost of failing, not the number of things at stake.
- **Stakes have a face.** A world-ending threat stays abstract until it is tied to one character the
  player has met and the specific thing they lose (Chu, Writing Excuses). The adjacent evidence comes
  from charity research: people give more to one identifiable victim than to statistics, and adding
  statistics to the victim *reduced* giving (Small, Loewenstein & Slovic, 2007). Don't stack casualty
  counts on top of the face.
- **No fridging**: a loved one's death is not the default stakes source (Writing Excuses homework).
- **Earn the scale.** A jump from nobody to saving the world within a few chapters feels unearned
  (Sanderson, 2025 lectures).

## Setups and payoffs

- **The ledger**: every prominent setup is paid off, cut, or deliberately acknowledged. Chekhov's
  letter of 1 November 1889 warns against an unfired rifle on stage — and *The Cherry Orchard* holds
  two firearms that never fire, so the rule is a ledger, not a law. (The pistol version is Gurliand's
  recollection, published in 1904.)
- **Promise, progress, payoff** — including the tonal promises an opening makes (Sanderson).
- **Coincidence**: coincidences that get characters *into* trouble are fine; coincidences that get
  them *out* are cheating (Emma Coats's 2011 tweets as a Pixar story artist — hers, not "Pixar's
  rules").
- **No machine from the ceiling**: the resolution arises from the plot (Aristotle). In a game, every
  way out of a major jam uses a capability, relationship or choice the player already has.

## Theme

Egri's premise (a value in conflict leading to an outcome), McKee's controlling idea (the value that
wins at the climax and the cause that makes it win) and Truby's moral argument (carried by the hero's
choices) agree: theme lives in action and outcome, not in anyone's mouth. A story becomes a sermon
when the argument outweighs the plot (Truby).

- **Theme question**: a question with two defensible answers.
- **Controlling idea**: the answer the climax enacts. Nobody states it.
- **The other answer, embodied**: at least one major character lives the opposing answer through what
  they do (`references/cast.md` → *Stance grid*).
- **Two plus two**: let the player assemble the meaning (Stanton). Save the Cat's "theme stated" line,
  spoken *to* the hero early by someone else, is the documented exception.
- **Branching**: each ending has its own controlling idea, and every ending answers the same question.

## The order of telling

What happened (fabula) and the order the player learns it (syuzhet) are separate design layers, and
the second sets the emotion. STORY.md keeps them apart: Truth, Tellings, Reveals.

- Postponing an outcome produces **suspense**; showing an outcome before its causes produces
  **curiosity**; an unexpected event produces **surprise** (Brewer & Lichtenstein; the curiosity part
  has since been questioned).
- An open question pulls the player forward (Loewenstein's information gap).
- **The bomb under the table**: telling the player about the threat in advance turns an ordinary scene
  into sustained suspense (Hitchcock). Keep surprise for reveals that *are* the payoff.
- **A fair twist** is contrary to expectation yet caused by what came before (Aristotle): at least two
  earlier beats support it on a second pass.
- The spoiler research is mixed (one study found spoiled stories enjoyed more; two others found the
  opposite), so a twist is never the story's only source of engagement.

## Arc shapes are vocabulary

Sentiment arcs of 1,327 Gutenberg books fell into six shapes; the most downloaded were Icarus, Oedipus
and a *double* man-in-a-hole (fall, rise, fall, rise) — not the simple man-in-a-hole often quoted
(Reagan et al., 2016; downloads are a rough proxy, and an emotional arc is not a plot). Use the shapes
to name an intended arc — a tragic Icarus for a companion, a falling Oedipus for a villain — and
choose deliberately: models put over half their stories into a man-in-a-hole and almost never write
tragedy (Tian et al., 2024). The per-beat value turn, not the curve, is the audit.

## Checks

1. **Deletion**: every beat, deleted, changes a later beat.
2. **Links**: "but" or "therefore" fits between consecutive beats of a strand.
3. **Turn**: every beat's Change names a value and its charge, entry to exit.
4. **Face**: every large threat has one met character and one specific loss.
5. **Ledger**: every setup paid, cut or acknowledged; every exit from a major jam uses something the
   player has; no coincidence gets anyone out of trouble.
6. **Theme**: a question with two answers; the climax enacts one; a major character lives the other;
   nobody states the lesson.
7. **Twists**: two earlier supporting beats each; never the only engagement.
8. **Arc**: chosen, not defaulted — a tragic, bittersweet or falling option was considered.
