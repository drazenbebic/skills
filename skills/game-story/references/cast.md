# Cast: characters, arcs, ensembles, antagonists

Characters are wants under pressure. This file covers who they are; how they *speak* belongs to
game-dialogue's voice profiles, which read these entries as canon. Several frameworks here are known
through reader notes rather than the books, and say so.

## The character core

A small core recurs across the frameworks:

- **Want** — the conscious goal, visible in play.
- **Weakness** — psychological (hurts only them) or moral (hurts others) (Truby). One primary desire
  line per protagonist.
- **Lie and need** — the false belief that blocks them, and the truth that disproves it, often reached
  only by giving up the want (Weiland's wound → Lie → want → need).
- **Dimensions known, not shown** — physiology, sociology, psychology are the writer's notes, surfaced
  through behaviour (Egri).
- **True character shows in a choice under pressure** (McKee). Every core character gets one on screen.
- **A signature** — one concrete detail that is only theirs: a habit, an object, a rule they keep, a
  job done their way. It is what a player remembers first, and the voice profile grows from it.

**The deletion test** for backstory: if removing it leaves present behaviour unexplained, it is
structural; if it only explains a trait the player already sees, it's decoration. Parul Sehgal's 2021
essay argues trauma has become fiction's all-purpose motive, reducing character to symptom; her
critics answer that the target is trauma used as *explanation*, not trauma as subject.

**Specific beats generic.** Kasavin built Hades' Demeter from one myth — a mother who lost this
particular daughter — and that single fact shaped her bitterness and the angle of her powers.

No study measures what makes a game character memorable. The proxies a bible can record are the ones
above, plus what the player should feel about them.

## Arcs

Weiland's taxonomy: **positive** (Lie → truth); **flat** (the hero holds the truth and changes others,
under a doubt that never wins); and three negative arcs — **disillusionment**, **fall**,
**corruption**.

- **Games often put the arc on the cast.** A flat-arc protagonist suits a silent, customisable,
  returning or repeating (roguelite) hero. The change then moves to companions who each hold a version
  of the Lie, to a faction, or to the antagonist — name who holds it and at which beat each is
  converted, broken or lost.
- **A protagonist's change arc needs a lever.** At least one player choice can push it, or it is an arc
  the player watches happen to someone else.
- **Betrayal needs played warmth first.** Insomniac's Spider-Man turns Peter's mentor into the
  antagonist around the second-act break; the played relationship before it gives the turn its weight
  (Barelli's analysis).

## The ensemble

A cast is a web of different answers to one question.

- **Truby's character web** sets characters by function, archetype, theme and opposition, including the
  fake-ally opponent and fake-opponent ally (reader notes). **Four-corner opposition**: the hero against
  a main opponent and at least two secondary opponents, all in conflict with one another, as different
  as possible (secondary summaries).
- **Companions disagree.** Laidlaw describes dividing Dragon Age's companions among writers and building
  each to react to the central conflict with a different opinion, so none stood in for a single
  philosophy. New Vegas's factions feel real because their members disagree and challenge each other
  (Sawyer).
- **Distinct at a glance.** Archetypal personalities let players tell a large cast apart at once, and a
  new character must serve the story and fit the existing cast (Kasavin). Hades' cast shares one frame —
  a big dysfunctional family — with each member taking a different stance within it.
- **Bonds wired to systems.** Persona's Confidants tie friendships to perks in other systems (fusion
  bonuses, discounts, battle skills); Mass Effect 2's loyalty missions resolve a squadmate's conflict and
  decide whether they survive the finale.

### Stance grid

State the theme question; give each core character a one-line answer and a method. Merge or change any
two who answer the same way by the same method. Language models write friendly casts — relationship
signs averaging positive where human fiction's run slightly negative (Nonaka & Perry, preprint) — so
check for **persistent adversarial ties**, at least one of which survives the ending unreconciled.

### Companion checklist

- A want independent of the main quest.
- At least one likely player choice they will oppose.
- An opinion of at least one other companion — a web, not spokes to the player.
- An arc beat the player can affect.
- A mechanical hook (a perk, a skill, a combat role, a fusion, a shop).

### Tiers

No source gives a cast size. Tier every named character — **core**, **recurring**, **functional**,
**ambient** — and scale the core to the content budget per character (scenes, lines, voice).

## Antagonists

The best-sourced rule: the strongest opponent **wants the same goal as the hero** and is exceptionally
good at **attacking the hero's greatest weakness** (Truby, via reader notes). In Barelli's reading of
Spider-Man, Otto and Peter both want justice for the city and Norman held to account, but Otto will
sacrifice innocents and Peter won't — so Otto's plan turns Peter's morality into a liability.

- **Give an abstract threat a face.** The same game's plague is Otto's weapon. A corruption, a static, a
  blight needs a person or faction who wields, worships or profits from it.
- **A plan that moves without the player.** A tabletop *front* pairs each danger with an impending doom
  and a sequence of grim portents that advance whether or not the players act — one to three portents
  for an adventure front, three to five for a campaign front, at most three dangers per front (Dungeon
  World). Keep the doom sinister but short of world-ending so the player doesn't feel they have no
  choice (Sly Flourish).
- **Rivals shift with taste.** Masuda gave three reasons Pokémon rivals became friendlier: pixel art had
  pushed personality into harsh dialogue, HD would make a jerk rival far more intense, and in his view
  players accept such personalities less. Critics answered that friendly, easily beaten rivals lost
  their tension.
- **Presence wears.** Borderlands 2's villain joked even through personal tragedy and grew tiresome;
  later passes gave him a breaking point (Burch).

### The antagonist sheet

- The **shared goal**, and the **method** the hero refuses.
- The hero's **weakness** they exploit, and how.
- A one-line **counter-argument** on the theme that a reasonable player could find tempting.
- A **front**: 3–5 portents, each with what happens if the player does nothing, and the beat that shows
  it.
- For an abstract threat: the **face** who wields it.

A model left to itself softens every one of these fields — the method blurs into menace, the argument
into "misunderstood", the ending into redemption. Check them again at the climax: the antagonist still
has a method and a counter-argument when the player meets them last.

## Supporting roles

Mentors, guides, quest-givers, vendors and hub characters each get a want and an attitude, even when
unnamed. Hub characters carry story when they react to progress — a new line after each gate, a
changed shop, an absence. Their lines are game-dialogue's; their wants, what they know and when they
change are this file's.

## Checks

1. **Core**: every core character has a want, a typed weakness, a Lie or doubt, one pressured choice
   on screen, and a signature drawn from the owner's material or a named source.
2. **Backstory**: every past passes the deletion test, and is specific.
3. **Arc**: chosen per character; for a flat protagonist, who changes instead and where.
4. **Lever**: a protagonist's change arc can be pushed by a player choice.
5. **Stances**: no two core characters answer the theme question the same way by the same method.
6. **Friction**: persistent adversarial ties exist; at least one survives the ending.
7. **Companions**: every item on the checklist.
8. **Antagonist**: the whole sheet filled — and intact at the last encounter.
9. **Face**: every abstract threat has one.
10. **Tiers**: every named character tiered; the core fits the budget.
