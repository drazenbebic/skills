# World: rules, factions, history, places

A world earns its space in the bible by creating decisions and conflicts. Everything else is depth
the writers may know and the player need not see. Sources are named briefly so a rule can be traced.

## Know more, show less

- **The iceberg**: know far more than you show (Hemingway). Put on screen only the rules, places and
  history that create a decision or a conflict for someone.
- **The case against worldbuilding**: M. John Harrison's 2007 attack on it as "the great clomping foot
  of nerdism" argues that it literalises invention and does the reader's imagining for them. The
  synthesis that survives both him and the worldbuilders is the iceberg. "Worldbuilder's disease" is a
  folk term with no traceable origin.
- **Optional depth goes to optional channels** — codex, items, environment — and never contradicts
  canon, because dataminers read everything (`references/game.md` → *Delivery modes*).
- **Don't build family trees before the plot** (Blind Squirrel's narrative guide): high-level features
  first, detail where a beat needs it.

## Rules that cost something

Sanderson's laws treat world rules as tools whose limits create plot:

- **First law** (2007 essay): an author's ability to resolve conflict with magic is directly
  proportional to how well the reader understands it. The same essay places soft and hard magic at the
  two ends of a continuum.
- **Second law**: limitations are more interesting than powers. Separate **limitations** (what it can't
  do), **weaknesses** (what enemies exploit) and **costs** (what it takes from the user).
- **Third law**: expand what you have before adding something new.

For a game, "magic" is any rule the story leans on — a fusion process, a contagion, a vehicle's fuel, a
curse, a guild licence. Each rule in the bible names its limitation or cost and the decision it forces
on someone. If the player must understand it to solve something, it is **taught before it resolves
anything**, or the climax becomes an unearned rescue.

## Factions

A faction is real when it wants something another faction can't also have.

- A **goal** incompatible with at least one other faction's.
- A **contested resource** — land, water, a creature, a secret, the player's loyalty.
- **Internal dissent**: members who disagree and challenge each other (New Vegas, Sawyer).
- A **stance** on the theme question — a different one from its rivals.

A faction with no rival, no dissent or no stance is set dressing; give it one or fold it into another.

## History as present pressure

History earns its place by naming the pressure it puts on the present: a **grudge**, a **debt**, a
**ruin**, a **taboo**, a **contested claim**. Events that create none are tagged optional deep lore.

The record constrains plans. World of Warcraft's historians sometimes forced planned stories to change
— writers once planned a storyline for a character who had already died in a novel (Game Informer).
Check the Truth section before giving anyone a future.

## Places

- **Every place in the bible is used by a beat** — say which, and for what.
- **Hazards that change play** belong in the entry, so level design can use them (Blind Squirrel).
- **"Where am I?"** answered within about 15 seconds of arrival (Carson), and a governing story for
  each place that the whole team follows.
- **A real place carries more than an invented one.** Firewatch's forest, Mundaun's valley and Night in
  the Woods' Rust Belt towns came from places their makers knew (`references/particulars.md` →
  *Where particulars come from*).

## Checks

1. **Rules**: every world rule names its limitation or cost and the decision it forces.
2. **Teaching**: every rule that resolves something was taught before it did.
3. **Factions**: each has an incompatible goal, a contested resource, internal dissent and a stance.
4. **History**: each event names a present pressure, or is tagged optional.
5. **Places**: each is used by a beat; hazards recorded; arrival answers "where am I?".
6. **Lore**: nothing optional contradicts canon; nothing critical is only optional.
