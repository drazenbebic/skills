# Particulars: tone, sources, cultures, names, inspiration

What makes a story *this game's* story rather than its genre's: a tone agreed with the loop, details
that trace to a life, a procedure or a source, names that obey the world's own sounds, and an honest
account of what was borrowed. Sources are named briefly so a rule can be traced.

## Tone

The games praised for mixing tones pick one **dominant register**, tie it to **how the game plays**,
and **protect** certain moments from jokes.

- **Matched to the loop.** Kasavin: roguelikes have an inherently slapstick, comedic quality, so Hades'
  tone was meant to match its loop; early-access feedback confirmed the humour.
- **Protected moments.** Toby Fox checks that no serious scene is ruined by a joke — while admitting he
  may keep a really funny one on purpose. A strong default, not an absolute ban.
- **Dark, without misery fatigue.** Darkest Dungeon tuned crit chances, art, lighting and narration to
  one message — you are underpowered and vulnerable — with successes "commented on bemusingly" and the
  narrator saving his excitement for big hits. Tyler Sigman: "not a game for everyone", and committing
  to the tone widened its appeal.
- **Light, carrying weight.** Spiritfarer's spirits were built from the team's memories of people who
  had died, and the team met patients and carers in end-of-life care.
- **The documented failures are mismatches**: reflexive quips that tell the audience nobody cares (an
  opinion, often aimed at the "Marvel quip"); Forspoken's banter drawn from a handful of conversations
  and repeated until a setting was added; Uncharted 4's hidden "Ludonarrative Dissonance" trophy for a
  thousand kills, naming the gap between a charming hero and his body count.
- **Agree tone before writing.** In television's tone meeting, the writer walks the director through
  each scene's emotional and tonal moments (WGA East guide). Here, tag each major beat's tone.

**The tone contract** in the one-sheet: the dominant register; the gameplay feel it matches; the
moments no joke may touch; the story's attitude to the main loop. For a light game, at least one loss
handled with care; for a dark one, rationed triumphs, reversals and gallows wit.

## Where particulars come from

In every well-documented case, the specific material came first and the genre was fitted around it.
Three sources:

- **A life**: Celeste's anxiety was its creator's own; Firewatch is set in the Wyoming forest its
  writer grew up near, the year after the 1988 fires; Night in the Woods drew on the Pennsylvania towns
  its creator moved to at eighteen.
- **An observed procedure**: Papers, Please began with Lucas Pope watching what immigration inspectors
  did with paperwork.
- **Research and consultants**: Pentiment's historical advisers; Hellblade's work with a psychiatrist
  and people with lived experience of psychosis; Morteshka choosing Slavic folklore over generic elves
  and goblins and taking a tale from a named 19th-century collection.

Chekhov's real advice on detail was a specific image — the neck of a broken bottle glittering on a
mill dam — later compressed into the misattributed "glint of light on broken glass". The evidence on
originality itself is mixed (one study of hit songs found moderate distinctiveness paid; a larger one
found novelty hurt popularity), so aim at **particularity** — specific, motivated, load-bearing detail
— not novelty for its own sake.

**An agent has no life to draw on.** Its job is to draw particulars out of the owner, point to
research and consultants, and mark every specific detail it invents as `proposed`, a placeholder until
someone verifies it.

## Real cultures

Early, continuous and empowered involvement from the people depicted is the documented good practice;
consultation lowers risk but certifies nothing. A ladder, from the cases:

| Level | Means | Case |
|---|---|---|
| Desk research | Published sources only | — |
| Insider review | Members of the culture review the work | Ghost of Tsushima: reviewed from the pitch by Sony's Japan team; still criticised for stereotype |
| Insiders shape it | Elders and storytellers steer content | Never Alone: begun by the Cook Inlet Tribal Council, about 40 Alaska Native contributors, narration in Iñupiaq |
| Insiders decide | Final say with the community | When Rivers Were Trails, described as a "sovereign" game |

A publisher's statement that a game was never meant as factual history (Assassin's Creed Shadows) did
not defuse its controversy. Per borrowed culture, the bible records: the **specific source tradition**
(region, period, text or version); the **consultation level**; known **scholarly disagreements** and the
story's **deliberate departures**; and the **questions a consultant should answer**. Flag any culture
whose consultation level is low for how central it is. Never mark anything authentic. **Pastiche** —
several real cultures mixed into one fantasy faction without saying what came from where — is the
pattern to flag.

## Names

Two methods sit at opposite ends: Tolkien built languages first and invented stories to give them a
world; A. K. Larkwood's lighter method gives each culture a **consonant inventory, a syllable shape,
cluster rules and one spelling per sound**, so that the same sound is spelled differently by different
peoples and readers feel the cultures apart.

- **A naming spec per culture**, differing from every other culture's on at least two of those
  dimensions, with three examples.
- **Distinct at a glance**: no shared initials in the core cast, no rhymes or lookalikes, no first name
  echoing another character's surname. Editors' heuristics, disputed by some writers — but cheap to
  check.
- **A meaning gloss** for every invented creature, place and character name, so localisers can rebuild
  it. Pokémon names were rebuilt one by one for English to mean something (Bulbasaur, Ekans as "snake"
  reversed), while Pikachu kept its name because the trademark was secured worldwide (Ishihara).
- **A collision check**: against the cast, famous IP, trademarks, offensive words in the target
  languages, and UI length limits.
- **The spec beats a ban list.** Model-default names (Elias, Elara, Mara, Kael) obey no setting's
  phonology, so a name that fails the spec is a stronger signal than any list hit.

## Building on an inspiration

- **Ideas are free; expression is not.** U.S. copyright "does not protect facts, ideas, systems, or
  methods of operation", only how they are expressed (Copyright Office; 17 U.S.C. §102(b)); names,
  titles and short phrases aren't copyrightable, though trademark law may protect them (Circular 33).
- **Mechanics carry a different risk.** Nintendo and The Pokémon Company sued Pocketpair over Palworld
  in September 2024 for patent infringement, not copyright; it was unresolved as of November 2026.
  Palworld's creature designs also drew public accusations of tracing — a reputational cost whatever
  the legal outcome. Sources here are U.S. and Japan only; patent and trademark questions need a
  qualified reviewer.
- **Keep the feeling, change the expression.** Stardew Valley set out to recapture Harvest Moon's
  feeling; Cassette Beasts' team said theirs was "not a tribute or derivative", and built the fusion
  the genre lacked; Team Cherry chased Zelda II's sense of mystery, not its look; Yacht Club documented
  the NES rules Shovel Knight deliberately broke; Toby Fox wanted Undertale to match the Mother games'
  emotional power without copying their methods.

**The inspiration ledger**: what is kept (a feeling, a mechanic); what is changed; what the original
never did that this game does; and what was checked for collisions — names, creatures, characters,
setting — against the inspiration and famous IP. Originality is the owner's and a reviewer's to
confirm, never the bible's to claim.

## Pillars

A pillar is a short, concrete statement of what the game is about, used to cut and keep content.

- **It must be able to fail a scene.** God of War (2018) was built on Combat, Father/Son and
  Exploration; its side quests were written around Kratos teaching his son, and "if the quest didn't
  serve a definitive purpose, it didn't make it". (The studio describes writing to the pillar and
  cutting what failed it; giving a failing scene one rewrite toward the pillar before cutting is this
  skill's own policy.)
- **Design by subtraction**: Unpacking narrowed many experience adjectives to three pillars, then built
  without scores, fail states or dialogue.
- Narrative pillars complement the design pillars; tone is a separate field (Blind Squirrel). "Three to
  five pillars" is a blog heuristic.
- **Comparisons cut both ways.** One team dropped its "Sunless Sea meets Majora's Mask" pitch after
  angry comments online; "inspired by" wording did about as well.

## Checks

1. **Tone**: a dominant register tied to the loop; protected moments listed; major beats tagged.
2. **Particulars**: every specific detail traces to the owner, an observed procedure or a named
   source — or is marked `proposed`.
3. **Swap**: no premise, place or character could move to another game unchanged.
4. **Cultures**: each borrowed tradition has a source, a consultation level, departures and questions;
   nothing claims authenticity; no unlabelled mixing.
5. **Names**: every name obeys its culture's spec; no collisions; glosses for invented names.
6. **Inspiration**: the ledger is filled; nothing but feelings and mechanics is carried over; nothing
   claims to be original.
7. **Pillars**: each could fail a scene; every beat serves one.
