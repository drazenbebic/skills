# Craft: lines and scenes

Rules for what a line does and how a scene moves. Sources are named in brackets so a rule can be
traced; the project's own rules beat every default here.

## Want, tactic, status

A line is an **action**: one character trying to get something from another. Under every line sit a
desire, an intent and an action — together, a verbal tactic (McKee, *Dialogue*). Write the want first,
then the words.

- **Mamet's three questions** test any scene: *Who wants what? What happens if they don't get it? Why
  now?* No answer to all three, no scene.
- **Every line does at least two jobs**: carry information, reveal character, shift the relationship
  (Skolnick; Emily Short). A line that does one job is a candidate for cutting or merging; a line that
  does none is cut (Adams).
- **Status** is the pecking-order position people claim through behaviour, negotiated in every
  exchange; the quickest way to raise your own is to lower someone else's (Johnstone, *Impro*). Track
  who is up and who is down after each line. A scene with no status movement is a scene where nothing
  happens.
- **Characters over plot.** Players remember people far more than events (Abernathy & Rouse, GDC
  2014). Even an unnamed NPC gets an attitude and a want (Slabinski).
- **Villains need a method, not menace.** Name the lie, the leverage, the false concession, the
  threat to something specific. "Menacing" with no method comes out as shouting.

## Subtext

Subtext is the gap between what a character wants and the words they choose to get it. McKee splits
speech into the **said** (chosen to express), the **unsaid** (thought, not voiced) and the
**unsayable** (what they cannot name even to themselves). Good dialogue is mostly said, shaped by the
other two.

- **Fix the subtext, vary the text** (Ingold). In interactive scenes the power struggle underneath is
  the structure; the player chooses the surface wording.
- **Never explain what everyone in the world already knows**, and never state the theme aloud
  (Ingold).
- **The toolkit** for carrying what is unsaid:
  - a talkative character goes terse;
  - deflection into logistics ("Did you eat?");
  - oddly precise about a trivial detail;
  - an action in place of a reply;
  - a high-status character suddenly asking permission;
  - answering the feeling behind a question instead of the question;
  - repeating the listener's word back, changed;
  - a joke at the worst moment, as a shield (and only as a shield — see Humour).
- **Forbidden:** "I feel X because Y", characters summarising their own arc, two characters agreeing
  on what a scene meant.

## Exposition as ammunition

When a scene is full of conflict, characters use every fact they have against each other, and
information flows in unnoticed (McKee, *Story*). When conflict is missing, the writer falls back on
*table dusting* — two servants chatting purely to brief the audience.

- **"As you know, Bob"** — a character telling a listener what both already know — is the classic
  failure. Reroute the fact through someone who genuinely doesn't know, or turn it into a weapon.
- **The ignorant listener** is the cleanest carrier: a stranger, a child, a character out of their
  time asks, and the answer is for the player too.
- **Optional lore stays optional.** History, politics and cosmology go to codex entries, side quests
  and found documents; the main path carries only what the player needs now (Slabinski, crediting
  Vandeberghe).
- **Seed and drip.** Front-loading is a beginner's mistake; give barely enough and curiosity grows
  (Skolnick: under three minutes of exposition before GTA III hands over control).
- **Radio drama's lesson**: dialogue may carry what the player cannot see, but mood belongs to
  sound and staging, not to a character saying "it's hot".

## Emotion unsaid

Characters do not name their feelings (Nicklin; Ingold). Emotion arrives through:

- **register and rhythm** — a formal character breaking into short sentences;
- **what they do** — the bottle set down, the potion pressed into a hand, the door left open;
- **what they avoid** — the topic changed every time it comes near;
- **what they get wrong** — a slip, a wrong name, a correction that gives them away;
- **the other person's reaction** — "Don't look at me like that."

A bark can carry fear without saying "afraid": an enemy reporting it lost the player, too fast, too
loud (Redding on Far Cry 2). Behind all of it sits the iceberg: what the writer knows and leaves out
still strengthens what shows.

## Humour

Humour is a **tactic**, not a verbal tic (Adams, "6 tips for writing video game dialogue that's
actually funny").

- Get comedy from **character under pressure** in a situation that challenges their assumptions; a
  funny situation writes the jokes.
- **No quip machines.** If every character jokes, every voice is the same voice.
- **Stop being funny when the stakes turn.** A quip that deflates a sincere moment is the most
  criticised dialogue failure of recent years (Forspoken, Saints Row 2022 reviews).
- **Puns belong in meta text** — titles, item names — not in a character's mouth every scene.
- **Repetition kills jokes**: a line heard more than three times an hour tires; a joke turns to a
  groan within about two hours of repeats (Adams).
- **The joke test:** would *this* character, in *this* state, say it to *this* listener — and is it a
  tactic (deflecting pain, claiming status, defusing a threat) or a wink at the player? A wink fails.

## Length

Game lines compete with play, so they run shorter than film or prose. These are defaults; a
project's own length rule replaces them.

| Measure | Default | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Comics balloon | ≤ ~25 words; 35–50 a panel | Dark Horse guide; Moore citing Weisinger |
| Subtitle | 2 lines of ~38–42 characters ≈ 12–15 words | Hamilton; Netflix |
| Most NPC lines | 1–2 subtitle cards, ≈ 15–30 words | derived from the above |
| A first draft | cut to ½–⅓ of its length | Slabinski (62 words → 20) |
| Choice text | 1–5 words | Nicklin |
| Options per choice | about 3 | Ingold |

- People rarely talk in paragraphs (Nicklin). Read every line aloud.
- Save eloquence for climaxes — the speech before the last fight, not the shopkeeper's hello.
- Contractions by default; uncontracted speech marks a stiff, formal or alien character.
- Long lines are legitimate where the project chose them (a box the player reads at leisure, holding
  a hundred words). Long lines still need a want and a turn; length is a budget, not a licence.

## Scene shape

A conversation is a scene in miniature: a want, an obstacle, a turn, and something different at the
end.

1. **Open cold on the want** — no greeting, no small talk ("enter late").
2. **Surface the obstacle** or the counter-want.
3. **Two to four exchanges** of tactic and counter-tactic, each moving status or information.
4. **Turn** on a reveal, a refusal or a concession.
5. **Exit on an open question** or a short player choice ("leave early"). For each question answered,
   raise another (Slabinski).

An NPC with real stakes may get impatient, refuse, or end the conversation; endless patience while
the player clicks through every topic is the hub-and-spoke failure (Short). Cut the first and last
line of any draft and see whether it improves — usually it does.

## Dialect and period speech

- **Carry dialect in word choice and syntax, not spelling.** Heavy phonetic rendering makes players
  decode instead of follow, can read as mockery of real speakers, and breaks localization and screen
  readers (Leonard's seventh rule; LitReactor; MasterClass).
- **In voiced games, leave accent to the actor**; write pronunciations in the VO notes.
- **No default "thee and thou."** Fake-archaic speech is a cliché; archaism works as one character's
  quirk, not everyone's register (Slabinski; Hedlund).
- **Invented expletives** set a world apart ("frak"); one or two, used consistently.
- **Research below the waterline.** Collect far more jargon, idiom and period vocabulary than will
  appear; use only what a player can decode from context.
- **Modern idiom in a period setting** must be a deliberate choice, defended against the setting and
  the speaker — register errors are punished louder than prose errors.

## Before and after

**Exposition → ammunition**
- Before: "As you know, the Ashen Guild has controlled the river trade for forty years since the
  Treaty of Varn."
- After: "Forty years the Guild's bled this river. You want across? Pay them, or swim."
- The fact survives, attached to a threat and a want.

**Named feelings → subtext**
- Before: A: "I'm angry that you left us at the bridge." B: "I feel guilty about it."
- After: A: "Bridge held, by the way. In case you wondered." B sets a bottle down. "Brought you
  something. Keep it."
- Anger arrives as sarcasm, guilt as a peace offering; neither is named.

**Soft villain → method**
- Before: "You'll regret crossing me. I am more dangerous than you know."
- After: "Your sister signed for the loan. Lovely hand. Shall I show the magistrate, or will you
  sit down?"
- The menace is a specific piece of leverage, and the villain is polite while using it.

**Quest dump → ask**
- Before: "Long ago my grandfather built this farm with his own hands, and we have always kept goats,
  and lately something has been taking them at night, which is a great hardship, so if you could
  possibly look into it I would be ever so grateful, and I can pay."
- After: "Three goats in a week, and the fence never broken. Find what's climbing it. I'll pay in
  silver — and I won't ask how."
- Threat, task and reward in a third of the words, and the last clause gives the farmer a character.
